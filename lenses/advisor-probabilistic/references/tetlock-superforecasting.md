# Superforecasting — Philip E. Tetlock & Dan Gardner  ·  prefix [SF]
> Forecasting is a measurable, trainable skill; accuracy comes from *how* you think — probabilistic, granular, self-critical — not from credentials, ideology, or arcane math. Dominant lens when a decision hinges on judging the likelihood of an uncertain future event and you can keep score.

## Principles

### [SF:01] Optimistic skepticism  (ch 1)
- **Essence:** The world is partly clocklike (predictable) and partly cloud-like (chaotic). Foresight is real but bounded; it decays toward chance roughly three to five years out, yet useful prediction is achievable in the months-to-eighteen-months range.
- **Decision move:** Before forecasting, ask what *kind* of question this is — how far out, under what circumstances — and calibrate ambition to predictability rather than dismissing prediction wholesale or assuming certainty.
- **Applies / fails when:** Applies to bounded, recurring-enough events. Fails for genuinely chaotic, long-horizon, or first-of-kind events where even models can't beat the chimp.
- **Example:** Weather forecasts are reliable two days out, near-worthless past a week; the skill is knowing where that line falls for your question.

### [SF:02] Keep score to improve  (ch 1, 3, 8, 12)
- **Essence:** Forecasting improves only through the cycle forecast → measure → revise. Most forecasting has no measurement, so it never improves — like medicine before randomized trials. Brier scores (0 = perfect, 0.5 = chance, 2.0 = confidently wrong) make accuracy concrete and comparable.
- **Decision move:** Record forecasts with explicit terms, numbers, and timelines so they can be scored; review hits and misses against base rates and rivals to extract lessons.
- **Applies / fails when:** Requires unambiguous, falsifiable forecasts and enough of them; single vague forecasts can't be judged. Brier scores only have meaning relative to question difficulty and a benchmark (e.g., "always predict no change").
- **Example:** GJP beat the control group by 60–78% precisely because it measured relentlessly and fed results back into training.

### [SF:03] Doubt the tip-of-your-nose view  (ch 2)
- **Essence:** System 1 instantly produces coherent, confident answers from whatever is available (WYSIATI — What You See Is All There Is) and is insensitive to evidence quality. Coherence feels like truth but is not. The cure is doubt — engaging slow System 2 to interrogate the snap judgment.
- **Decision move:** Treat your first impression as a hypothesis, not a conclusion. Ask "What would convince me I'm wrong?" Slow down before committing on consequential calls.
- **Applies / fails when:** Snap intuition is valid in domains rich with learnable cues and fast feedback (firefighting, chess); it fails in low-validity domains (stock picking, geopolitics) where confident pattern-matching produces clear, compelling, and wrong impressions.
- **Example:** After the 2011 Oslo bombing, the plausible "Islamist terrorists" story was embraced instantly; the perpetrator was a lone anti-Muslim Norwegian.

### [SF:04] Beware bait-and-switch (attribute substitution)  (ch 2, 5)
- **Essence:** Faced with a hard question, the mind silently substitutes an easier one and answers that instead, then believes it answered the original.
- **Decision move:** Re-read the exact question. Confirm your answer responds to *it*, not to an adjacent emotional or simpler proxy.
- **Applies / fails when:** Universal risk; most dangerous when the substitute question is emotionally loaded.
- **Example:** "Will polonium be found in Arafat's remains?" gets swapped for "Did Israel poison Arafat?" — a different question entirely.

### [SF:05] Use numbers, not vague words  (ch 3, 6)
- **Essence:** Words like "serious possibility," "likely," "may" mean radically different odds to different people (Sherman Kent's analysts read "serious possibility" as anything from 20% to 80%). Numbers force metacognition and reduce ambiguity. "Better to be a bookie than a goddamn poet."
- **Decision move:** Express every forecast as an explicit numeric probability with a defined timeline and unambiguous terms. Ban "may," "might," "could" as standalone forecasts — they're unfalsifiable.
- **Applies / fails when:** Always preferable for scorable forecasts. Numbers should not be mistaken for objective fact — they remain subjective estimates, just explicit ones.
- **Example:** "Fair chance" of Bay of Pigs success meant 3:1 *against* to its author; Kennedy heard it as positive and approved a disaster.

### [SF:06] Defeat the wrong-side-of-maybe fallacy  (ch 3)
- **Essence:** People judge a probabilistic forecast by which side of 50% it fell on: a 70%-rain forecast is deemed "wrong" if it doesn't rain, though the forecast also said 30% no-rain. A single probabilistic forecast cannot be judged; only many can (via calibration).
- **Decision move:** Never grade one probabilistic call by its single outcome. Demand a track record. Resist the political incentive to hide behind elastic words to dodge this fallacy.
- **Applies / fails when:** Applies whenever evaluating probabilistic forecasts; the fallacy is what makes vague language safe and numbers risky for institutions.
- **Example:** When the Supreme Court upheld Obamacare after markets gave 75% to it being struck down, a sharp reporter wrongly declared "the crowd was wrong."

### [SF:07] Judge the decision, not the outcome  (ch 4)
- **Essence:** "Was it correct?" is a bait-and-switch for "Was it reasonable given what was knowable then?" A reasonable judgment can be wrong (Iraq WMD) and a good outcome can follow a bad decision (the lucky poker bet). Conflating the two corrupts learning.
- **Decision move:** Evaluate forecasts and decisions against the information available at the time, via mental time travel — not against later-revealed outcomes. Run postmortems on successes too.
- **Applies / fails when:** Essential under uncertainty and luck; the WMD failure was both reasonable *and* wrong — yet still correctable (no red teams, no devil's advocates, no consideration it might be wrong).
- **Example:** Every major intelligence agency thought Saddam was hiding WMDs because he *acted* like it; the conclusion was reasonable, wrong, and overconfident.

### [SF:08] Be a fox, aggregate perspectives (dragonfly eye)  (ch 3)
- **Essence:** Hedgehogs know one Big Idea and force everything through it (green-tinted glasses) — confident, media-friendly, and inaccurate, getting *worse* with more information. Foxes draw on many models and sources, talk in "howevers" and probabilities, and aggregate viewpoints like a dragonfly's compound eye. Foxes beat hedgehogs on both calibration and resolution. Fame correlates inversely with accuracy.
- **Decision move:** Deliberately gather multiple analytical lenses and independent information sources, then synthesize — including the "crowd within" (your own second, contrary estimate) and tweaking question wording to fight confirmation bias.
- **Applies / fails when:** Aggregation works best when inputs are diverse and at least somewhat informed; aggregating ignorance yields nothing. Note: the fox/hedgehog split is a spectrum, not a dichotomy.
- **Example:** Galton's fairgoers collectively guessed an ox's weight within one pound; errors canceled, valid signal accumulated.

### [SF:09] Fermi-ize the question  (ch 5)
- **Essence:** Break a seemingly impossible question into knowable and unknowable sub-questions ("What would have to be true for this to happen?"). This drags guessing into the light, separates what you know from what you're inventing, and yields surprisingly accurate estimates from crude inputs.
- **Decision move:** Decompose. Set 90% confidence intervals on each component, expose assumptions, dare to be wrong with explicit guesses, then assemble. Use hypotheses (each path to "yes") as the framework for the inside-view investigation.
- **Applies / fails when:** Works for quantifiable and apparently-unquantifiable questions alike; the discipline is decomposition, not precision of any single guess.
- **Example:** Estimating Chicago's piano tuners from population × ownership rate × tunings/year ÷ tuner work-hours lands near the real number.

### [SF:10] Outside view before inside view  (ch 5)
- **Essence:** Start with the base rate — how often things of this type happen in situations of this type — then adjust for case specifics. Diving into vivid case details first lets you anchor on a meaningless number; under-adjustment from a bad anchor produces bad estimates. The outside view supplies a meaningful anchor.
- **Decision move:** Find the relevant comparison class and its frequency first; set that as your starting probability; then move it up or down with case-specific (inside-view) evidence, conducted as a targeted investigation, not an aimless amble.
- **Applies / fails when:** Always lead with the outside view; refine the comparison class to match the case more tightly when possible (e.g., single-family-home pet ownership vs. all-household).
- **Example:** Estimate the Renzettis' chance of owning a pet by starting at the 62% US household rate, not by spinning a story about the family.

### [SF:11] Think probabilistically, in fine grains  (ch 6)
- **Essence:** Most people have a 3-setting dial — "yes / no / maybe" — a Stone Age heuristic. Scientists and superforecasters subdivide "maybe" into many grades. Granularity *predicts* accuracy: those who use single percentage points beat those who round to fives, who beat those who round to tens. Both 0% and 100% are rarely warranted; "nothing is one hundred percent."
- **Decision move:** Refuse to round to yes/no/maybe. Distinguish 60/40 from 55/45; debate whether it's 5% or 1% when consequences are large. Use as many degrees of doubt as the problem genuinely supports — but no more (false precision/bafflegab is also a failure).
- **Applies / fails when:** Granularity is real signal only when finer estimates track real frequency differences; otherwise it's bafflegab. People intuitively resist it outside familiar domains (sports) — a serious mistake elsewhere.
- **Example:** Obama collapsed a ~70% crowd estimate on bin Laden's location to "fifty-fifty," discarding usable information by retreating to the ignorance prior.

### [SF:12] Reject fate, embrace chance  (ch 6)
- **Essence:** "Everything happens for a reason" thinking treats improbable outcomes that occurred as having been certain (100%), which is incoherent and corrodes probabilistic reasoning. Fate-belief correlates with *worse* forecasting accuracy; probabilistic thinking with better.
- **Decision move:** Ask "How?" (physics, causation, probabilities), not "Why?" (metaphysics, meaning). Treat even identity-defining events as quasi-random draws from once-possible worlds.
- **Applies / fails when:** Finding meaning aids wellbeing but undermines foresight — a genuine tradeoff; this book optimizes for accuracy, not happiness.
- **Example:** Superforecasters scored lowest of all groups on a "fate score," and lower fate scores tracked lower (better) Brier scores.

### [SF:13] Update often, in small steps  (ch 7)
- **Essence:** Good forecasting is Bayesian in spirit: blend the prior with the diagnostic value of new evidence, capturing both old and new information. The dangers are under-reaction (ignoring new info) and over-reaction (overweighting a single noisy signal). The winning style is many small, incremental updates, with the rare willingness to jump hard or rebuild when a forecast rests on a now-broken assumption.
- **Decision move:** Watch developments closely; nudge probabilities in small increments on subtle diagnostic signals; sift signal from noise; jump only on genuinely diagnostic shocks; throw out and rebuild when the basis of a forecast collapses.
- **Applies / fails when:** Small updates are the default and usually right; but rigidly applying "many small updates" is wrong when the forecast's foundation is failing (dump it, re-anchor). You need not compute Bayes' theorem — grasp its core insight.
- **Example:** Tim Minto won a season averaging 3.5% updates over 34 changes on Syrian refugees (Brier 0.07); Doug Lorch's single jump from 55% to 95% on a stale sea-ice report blew up.

### [SF:14] Guard against belief perseverance  (ch 7)
- **Essence:** People rationalize away disconfirming evidence to protect settled beliefs, especially when beliefs are publicly committed or tied to identity (the Jenga tower: pull a load-bearing belief and the structure threatens to collapse, so people refuse to even imagine being wrong).
- **Decision move:** Notice when a belief is load-bearing for your identity or reputation — that's where you'll under-update. Lower your ego investment in each forecast; treat beliefs as hypotheses, not treasures.
- **Applies / fails when:** Strongest where commitment is public and identity-linked (Dan Kahan's work: risk views driven by identity, not evidence). Superforecasters' amateur status — low ego per forecast — is an advantage for updating.
- **Example:** Officials who imprisoned 112,000 Japanese-Americans argued the *absence* of sabotage *proved* sabotage was coming — extreme commitment producing extreme rationalization.

### [SF:15] Perpetual beta: try, fail, analyze, adjust, try again  (ch 8)
- **Essence:** Skill comes from informed practice with clear, timely feedback. A growth mindset (abilities are built by effort) plus grit (passionate perseverance) is the strongest predictor of becoming a superforecaster — roughly three times more powerful than intelligence. "75% perspiration, 25% inspiration."
- **Decision move:** Forecast a lot, get unambiguous scored feedback, run unflinching postmortems ("where exactly did I go wrong?"), and keep iterating. Don't kid yourself with vague language (Forer effect) or let hindsight bias rewrite your past judgments.
- **Applies / fails when:** Requires clear, prompt feedback (why meteorologists and bridge players are well-calibrated but police lie-detection and long-horizon pundits are not). Tacit skill can't be learned from lectures alone (Polanyi's bicycle).
- **Example:** Keynes, "consistently inconsistent," lost fortunes in 1920 and 1929, each time reanalyzed his assumptions, and ended wealthy with a transformed method.

### [SF:16] Build truth-seeking superteams  (ch 9)
- **Essence:** Groups can be wise or mad. They go wrong via groupthink and loss of independent judgment (Bay of Pigs); they go right via psychological safety, constructive confrontation, precision questioning, and information sharing (Cuban missile crisis). Superteams were 50% more accurate than individuals and beat prediction markets by 15–30%. Diversity of perspective often trumps raw ability; "givers" lift the whole team.
- **Decision move:** Form small diverse teams; make criticism explicitly welcome ("I want push-back"); use precision questioning to dissect vague claims; share information generously; use premortems; aggregate independent judgments before discussing to preserve independence.
- **Applies / fails when:** Works when independence is preserved and dissent is safe; fails into groupthink when agreement is taken as proof of correctness. A team's open-mindedness is an emergent property of communication patterns, not just member traits. Extremizing the aggregate helps only when the team is *not* already sharing all info.
- **Example:** Edmondson's best surgical teams let a nurse correct the surgeon; superteams said "our" more than "my."

### [SF:17] Lead with intellectual humility plus decisive action (mission command)  (ch 10)
- **Essence:** The apparent contradiction — superforecasters are humble and self-critical, leaders must be confident and decisive — dissolves under *Auftragstaktik*: tell subordinates the goal (the *why* and *what*), not the *how*. Intellectual humility (reality is complex, judgment is fallible) is not self-doubt and coexists with confidence in one's abilities. Deliberate slowly, then implement boldly; hold the plan but abandon it when reality demands.
- **Decision move:** Separate deliberation from execution. Push decision authority to those facing the surprises. As a leader, foster a culture where unwelcome words are safe and out-of-the-box thinkers are promoted. "The bold move is the right move except when it's the wrong move."
- **Applies / fails when:** Works when goals are clear and front-line judgment is trusted (Wehrmacht tactics, Eisenhower, Petraeus, Amazon's "disagree and commit," 3M, Walmart). Fails under top-down micromanagement (Hitler holding the Normandy reserves).
- **Example:** Annie Duke: humility "in the face of the game" (it's unmasterable) is different from humility "in the face of opponents" (she's confident she'll win) — both can be true at once.

### [SF:18] Prepare for fat tails and surprise  (ch 11)
- **Essence:** Cognitive illusions (WYSIATI, Müller-Lyer) can't be switched off, only monitored. Many consequential events are "gray swans" (imaginable, foreseeable in principle) rather than truly inconceivable black swans, and their consequences unfold through forecastable smaller steps. Historical outcomes follow fat-tailed, not bell-curve, distributions — extreme events are far likelier than normal-distribution intuition suggests.
- **Decision move:** For horizons beyond the forecasting limit (~5 years), plan for surprise: build resilience and adaptability, run worst-case thought experiments. Make the implicit probability judgments behind long-term planning explicit so they can be scrutinized. "Plans are useless, but planning is indispensable."
- **Applies / fails when:** Long-horizon geopolitical/economic forecasting (10+ years) is near-worthless beyond the obvious; resist 20-year forecasts. But "only black swans matter" is itself a false dichotomy — slow incremental change (1–2% annual growth, rising life expectancy) is profoundly important too.
- **Example:** A 1914 planner using a normal distribution would deem a 70-million-death war absurd; the true fat-tailed distribution makes it thousands of times more probable — worth trying to avert.

### [SF:19] Cluster small scorable questions into big ones (Bayesian question clustering)  (ch 12)
- **Essence:** The big questions ("How does this all turn out?") matter but can't be scored; small questions can be scored but seem trivial. Resolve the dilemma by decomposing a big question into many small, pertinent, answerable ones whose cumulative answers approximate the big picture — pointillism.
- **Decision move:** When facing an unscorable strategic question, generate a cluster of concrete, time-bounded, scorable sub-questions; track them; let the pattern of yeses/noes paint the answer.
- **Applies / fails when:** Works when the big question genuinely decomposes into observable events; pairs forecasting (answers) with good question-asking (the "smack-the-forehead" test) — the superforecaster and superquestioner are likely different mindsets that must collaborate (Tom-Bill symbiosis).
- **Example:** "How does Korea turn out?" becomes will-NK-test-a-rocket, will-it-test-a-nuke, will-it-shell-the-South — each yes nudges the big answer toward "badly."

### [SF:20] Don't treat commandments as commandments  (Appendix)
- **Essence:** Every guideline above requires balancing opposing errors (under- vs. over-reaction, prudence vs. decisiveness, learning too little vs. too much from failure). There are no binding rules because "two cases will never be exactly the same." Mastery is an error-balancing bicycle learned by doing, not by reading.
- **Decision move:** Apply the principles with constant mindfulness and contextual judgment; ask "which error would be worse in the situation I face?" Break a rule sooner than produce a barbarous forecast.
- **Applies / fails when:** The meta-principle governs all the rest; rigid rule-following defeats the purpose, especially when you're dutifully trying to follow the rules.
- **Example:** Triage (commandment 1) itself requires judgment — forecasting the 2028 election is futile, the 2016 field can at least be narrowed; only effort reveals which questions sit in the Goldilocks zone.
