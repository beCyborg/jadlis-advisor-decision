# Noise: A Flaw in Human Judgment — Kahneman, Sibony & Sunstein  ·  prefix [NSE]
> Wherever there is judgment, there is noise — undesirable variability in judgments that should be identical — and usually more of it than you think. Use this lens whenever interchangeable experts (or one expert on different occasions) produce different verdicts on the same case: hiring, sentencing, underwriting, medical diagnosis, forecasting, performance review, any repeated professional judgment.

## Principles

### [NSE:01] Bias vs Noise (Two Kinds of Error)  (Intro)
- **Essence:** Error has two independent components. Bias is the average error — shots systematically off the bull's-eye in one direction. Noise is the scatter — variability among shots that should land in the same place. You can measure noise from the *back* of the target, knowing nothing about the true value, because it is variability among judgments that ought to agree.
- **Decision move:** Diagnose which error you face. If everyone leans the same way, you have bias; if interchangeable people disagree, you have noise. Treat them with different tools and don't let a focus on bias hide the noise.
- **Applies / fails when:** Applies to any judgment with a notional correct answer. The "back of the target" trick fails for matters of pure taste where there is no shared target.
- **Example:** Five teams shoot at hidden targets; you can't tell who is biased, but you can instantly see which teams are noisy by how spread their shots are.

### [NSE:02] Wherever There Is Judgment, There Is Noise  (ch 1–4)
- **Essence:** Professionals expected to be interchangeable produce shockingly different verdicts on identical cases — federal judges' sentences for the same crime differ by years; insurance underwriters' premiums differ ~5x more than executives assume. Leaders operate under an "illusion of agreement," confident a second expert would say roughly the same thing.
- **Decision move:** Assume noise is present in any repeated professional judgment until measured. Never assume two of your experts would converge on the same case.
- **Applies / fails when:** Applies to high-volume interchangeable-judgment systems (claims, hiring, grading, diagnosis). Less relevant where one designated person owns a unique mandate.
- **Example:** A noise audit at an insurance company found a median 55% difference between underwriters on the same risk; leadership had guessed 10%.

### [NSE:03] Singular Decisions Are Noisy Too  (ch 3)
- **Essence:** It is tempting to think one-off decisions (a unique acquisition, a pandemic response) can't be "noisy" since they happen once. But a singular decision is just "a recurrent decision made only once": it sits in a cloud of counterfactual judgments the same person/group would have made under slightly different conditions. The same psychological mechanisms that scatter repeated judgments operate here.
- **Decision move:** Apply noise-reduction discipline (structure, independence, outside view) to big one-off decisions, not just routine ones — they are *more* exposed, not less.
- **Applies / fails when:** Applies to consequential strategic/unique decisions. The instinct to exempt them ("each case is unique") is exactly the trap.
- **Example:** Two equally competent strategy teams handed the same one-time acquisition would likely reach different recommendations.

### [NSE:04] Judgment ≠ Taste  (ch 4)
- **Essence:** A judgment is a measurement that aims at a true value (an evaluative or predictive verdict), not a statement of personal preference. Saying "it's a matter of judgment" rightly excuses small disagreement — but not large divergence. Some judgments are simply wrong, and expecting bounded agreement among professionals is legitimate.
- **Decision move:** Distinguish "matter of taste" (no shared target, disagreement fine) from "matter of judgment" (shared target, large disagreement = a problem worth measuring).
- **Applies / fails when:** Use to push back on the cop-out that all professional disagreement is acceptable subjectivity.
- **Example:** Two interviewers rating the same candidate opposite extremes aren't expressing taste — they're revealing noise in a judgment that has a real target (job performance).

### [NSE:05] The Error Equation  (ch 5)
- **Essence:** Overall (mean squared) error decomposes as Error² = Bias² + Noise². Mathematically, reducing bias or noise by the same amount improves accuracy equally. Crucially, reducing noise helps *regardless* of what you know about bias — you don't need to know the true value to know that less scatter is better.
- **Decision move:** Even when you can't measure or correct bias, attack noise: it is a guaranteed, no-regret accuracy gain.
- **Applies / fails when:** Holds under a squared-error (quadratic) loss, the standard for measurement. The symmetry is exact, not metaphorical.
- **Example:** When judgments split 84/16 above vs below the true value, bias roughly equals noise in their contribution to error.

### [NSE:06] Decompose System Noise: Level + Pattern  (ch 6)
- **Essence:** System Noise² = Level Noise² + Pattern Noise². **Level noise** = stable differences in how high/low judges set their average (some "hanging," some "bleeding-heart"). **Pattern noise** = the judge×case interaction: idiosyncratic reactions to particular cases (one judge harsh on white-collar crime, lenient on accomplices). In the sentencing study the two contributed about equally. Pattern noise also contains a transient slice (occasion noise).
- **Decision move:** When you find noise, decompose it: level noise can be fixed by recalibrating scales/anchors; pattern noise needs structure and aggregation. Knowing the mix targets the remedy.
- **Applies / fails when:** Requires the same judges to evaluate multiple cases to separate the components. Pattern noise is the larger, more stubborn enemy — invisible and hard to predict.
- **Example:** Judge 3 was 2 years more lenient on average (level) yet unusually harsh on Case C specifically (pattern).

### [NSE:07] Occasion Noise  (ch 7)
- **Essence:** The same person judges the same case differently on different occasions — driven by mood, fatigue, hunger, weather, what was just discussed, even the order of cases. "You are not the same person at all times." It's real but typically the *smallest* component of system noise (you differ less from yesterday's you than from a colleague today).
- **Decision move:** Reduce occasion noise by averaging your own judgment across occasions, standardizing conditions, and using checklists/structure so transient states matter less. Distrust a verdict made when tired or rushed.
- **Applies / fails when:** Matters most for high-stakes single judgments made under variable conditions. Don't over-index on it relative to between-person noise.
- **Example:** Judges grant parole more readily right after a meal break than just before one.

### [NSE:08] Groups Amplify Noise (Cascades)  (ch 8)
- **Essence:** Group deliberation often *increases* noise rather than averaging it out. Who speaks first, early popularity, and social proof trigger information and reputational cascades that push the whole group toward an arbitrary outcome — and produce false confidence and unity. Two groups discussing the same issue can end up firmly committed to opposite conclusions.
- **Decision move:** Collect judgments *independently before* discussion. Don't let one voice anchor the room; beware the confident consensus a group produces about itself.
- **Applies / fails when:** Applies to any committee/board/jury. The danger rises with status differences and open sequential speaking.
- **Example:** Music-download experiments: songs' success depended overwhelmingly on early arbitrary popularity, not intrinsic quality, once people saw others' choices.

### [NSE:09] Models Beat Humans Because They Are Noise-Free  (ch 9–10)
- **Essence:** Simple rules, formulas, and algorithms reliably outpredict expert human judgment — and the main reason is not superior insight but *noiselessness*. A mechanical model of a judge (a regression replicating the judge's own weights) often beats the judge, because it applies the rule consistently while the human is inconsistent. Equal-weight models do nearly as well as optimally fitted ones, and far better than case-by-case judgment.
- **Decision move:** Where you lack outcome data, build an equal-weight checklist/scoring rule and follow it. Override only for a genuine "broken leg" (a decisive, rare, observable fact the model can't see) — not merely because you dislike the output.
- **Applies / fails when:** Holds across many prediction domains (Meehl's finding, 60+ years on). Override discipline is essential — most "exceptions" are just discomfort, not broken legs.
- **Example:** A noise-free model of a clinician's judgment predicts outcomes more accurately than the clinician herself.

### [NSE:10] Objective Ignorance & the Illusion of Validity  (ch 11–12)
- **Essence:** There is a hard ceiling on predictive accuracy set by *objective ignorance* — the future is genuinely unknowable, and uncertainty grows the further out you look. People confuse the subjective feeling of confidence (an internal signal of coherence) with actual validity. Most events are neither expected nor surprising — they sit in the "valley of the normal" and feel explicable only after the fact. Typical real-world correlations are tiny (~.20, ~56% prediction accuracy).
- **Decision move:** Treat strong internal conviction as no evidence of accuracy. Check whether your experts beat chance. Be humble about long-horizon forecasts and resist after-the-fact "it was obvious" narratives.
- **Applies / fails when:** Applies hardest to long-term, complex social/economic/geopolitical prediction. Don't mistake the limit of knowledge for a fixable noise/bias problem.
- **Example:** "Correlation does not imply causation, but causation does imply correlation" — yet the correlations underlying confident expert prediction are routinely near .20.

### [NSE:11] Run a Noise Audit  (ch 2, App A)
- **Essence:** A noise audit measures how much interchangeable professionals disagree by having them independently judge the same realistic cases, then decomposing the variability into level and pattern noise. It shatters the illusion of agreement and quantifies the cost. Process matters: enlist the unit's own credible experts as authors, secure leadership's *advance* commitment to act on results, anonymize, and avoid the word "noise" with participants (call it a "decision-making study").
- **Decision move:** Before redesigning any judgment process, audit it: pick a numeric, paper-based judgment task; have all qualified professionals judge identical cases independently; record executives' prior expectations and acceptable-disagreement thresholds first.
- **Applies / fails when:** Works where judgments can be made on written info and expressed numerically. Credibility hinges on hostile-scrutiny-proof case design and pre-committed leadership.
- **Example:** The insurance audit: executives expected ~10% disagreement; the measured median was 55%, costing the firm hundreds of millions.

### [NSE:12] Debiasing Is Hard; Prefer Decision Hygiene  (ch 19)
- **Essence:** Debiasing targets a *named, directional* bias (ex post correction, or ex ante nudges/boosts) — but in any complex decision multiple biases act, in unknown directions, often offsetting unpredictably. That residual is noise. Decision hygiene is the analogue of handwashing: a set of procedures that prevent an *unspecified* range of errors without knowing which germ you stopped. Its victories are invisible and thankless — and worth it.
- **Decision move:** When the direction of error is unknown, stop hunting individual biases and install decision-hygiene procedures (independence, structure, aggregation, outside view, sequencing). Where a bias is known and robust (e.g. planning fallacy), still debias directly. Consider a trained, mandate-backed **decision observer** with a custom bias checklist to catch biases in real time (App B).
- **Applies / fails when:** Debiasing fits known strong biases; hygiene fits everything else. A self-appointed decision observer "will neither win friends nor influence people" — the role needs leadership backing.
- **Example:** Adding a buffer to a project estimate (debiasing the planning fallacy) can backfire if the team was actually loss-averse or already padded in anticipation.

### [NSE:13] Decompose into Independent Mediating Assessments  (ch 25)
- **Essence:** The Mediating Assessments Protocol (MAP) treats a big decision like a structured interview: "options are like candidates." Up front, break the decision into a short list of comprehensive, non-overlapping assessments; have them evaluated *independently* (different analysts, no cross-talk) to block the halo effect; each yields a fact-based rating answering "how strongly does the evidence on *this* topic argue for or against?" Independence keeps reality appropriately incoherent, surfacing real disagreement to debate.
- **Decision move:** MAP's six steps: (1) structure into mediating assessments; (2) use the outside view per assessment; (3) keep assessments independent in analysis; (4) review each separately in the meeting; (5) individual judgments first, then estimate-talk-estimate; (6) delay intuition, don't ban it.
- **Applies / fails when:** Applies whenever evaluating an option requires weighting multiple dimensions (acquisitions, hiring, strategy, investments). For recurring decisions, define the assessment list once.
- **Example:** "Mapco" scores an acquisition on ~7 independent assessments (financials, management quality, synergy likelihood, regulatory approval) before anyone voices an overall verdict.

### [NSE:14] Sequence Information (Need-to-Know)  (ch 20)
- **Essence:** Information that is irrelevant or biasing should be withheld from a judge until *after* the judgment that doesn't need it. Early exposure lets extraneous facts contaminate evaluation and creates bias cascades — a second opinion isn't independent if it knows the first. Even fingerprint examiners are swayed by knowing the case context.
- **Decision move:** Give each judge only what that specific judgment requires, in a deliberate order. Keep second/third opinions blind to prior ones to preserve independence.
- **Applies / fails when:** Powerful in forensics, diagnosis, review pipelines. Requires deliberately designing the information flow, not the default dump-everything brief.
- **Example:** Tell forensic examiners only the prints, not that detectives already suspect the defendant — context can flip the "match" verdict.

### [NSE:15] Aggregate Independent Judgments  (ch 21)
- **Essence:** Averaging multiple *independent* judgments is the most dependable noise-reduction tool: averaging N independent estimates cuts noise by a factor of √N (four judgments ≈ halve it). Independence is the precondition — correlated judgments don't average out. Superforecasters embody the complementary mindset: actively open-minded, in "perpetual beta," decomposing questions and relentlessly updating.
- **Decision move:** Collect several independent estimates and average them before discussing. Build the wisdom-of-crowds-within (your own multiple estimates) and across people. Foster perpetual-beta updating.
- **Applies / fails when:** The √N gain assumes genuine independence; group discussion (NSE:08) destroys it. Use estimate-talk-estimate to recover some benefit when discussion is unavoidable.
- **Example:** "Let's take the average of four independent judgments — that alone is guaranteed to cut noise in half."

### [NSE:16] Use Relative Judgments & Shared Scales  (ch 15, 23)
- **Essence:** Much noise comes from people using the *same words on different scales* — "good," "7/10," "high risk" mean different things to different raters (a major source of level noise). Relative judgments (ranking, comparing to concrete anchor cases) are far more reliable than absolute ratings on adjective/number scales, because comparison is something humans do well.
- **Decision move:** Replace absolute ratings with rankings or a **case scale** — anchor reference cases everyone knows ("as good as the team at ABC, not as good as DEF"). For performance/hiring, define the scale with concrete behavioral anchors, kept updated.
- **Applies / fails when:** Performance reviews, grading, hiring, any rating task. Anchor cases require up-front investment and must be shared and refreshed.
- **Example:** Performance ratings came out "one-quarter performance and three-quarters system noise" — fixable only by anchoring the scale in concrete cases.

### [NSE:17] Take the Outside View / Base Rate  (ch 11, 25)
- **Essence:** Start from the reference class — the statistics of similar cases (the base rate) — before adjusting for the specifics of *this* case. The inside view (building a story from case details) breeds overconfidence and the planning fallacy; the outside view anchors judgment in reality. "Before we discuss this, what is the relevant base rate?"
- **Decision move:** For every prediction/assessment, define a comparable reference class and get its base rate first; express the case as "above/below average" relative to that class; then adjust modestly.
- **Applies / fails when:** Essential for forecasts, project timelines, success probabilities. Hard when a good reference class is unclear — but even an imperfect one beats pure inside-view storytelling.
- **Example:** To assess regulatory approval odds, first find the % of comparable deals approved, then adjust for this deal's particulars.

### [NSE:18] Delay Intuition, Don't Ban It  (ch 25)
- **Essence:** Intuition is valuable but must come *last*. Forming a holistic gut verdict early causes premature closure and bends all subsequent evidence to confirm it (excessive coherence). The fix is to postpone the overall judgment until the independent, fact-based assessments are complete — then the informed intuition is anchored on evidence, not impressions. Banning intuition entirely backfires: people game rigid formulas and may miss genuine deal-breakers.
- **Decision move:** Sequence the meeting so the overall call is the *final* agenda item, after every component is assessed and discussed. Then explicitly invite holistic judgment — informed, not raw.
- **Applies / fails when:** Core to MAP. Don't replace judgment with a pure mechanical average: keep room for broken-leg exceptions and unanticipated deal-clinchers/breakers.
- **Example:** Mapco's board votes on its overall feeling about the deal only after all assessments are scored and debated — "we have delayed intuition; now is the time to use it."

### [NSE:19] Optimal Noise Is Not Zero  (ch 26–28)
- **Essence:** The right level of noise is low but not zero. Eliminating noise has real costs: it can be too expensive (five teachers per essay), undermine human **dignity** and the value of face-to-face human discretion, freeze **moral change** by locking in today's values, and reduce **deterrence** (a little uncertainty about penalties can magnify it). Rules cut noise but err on edge cases; standards allow adaptation but invite noise. Algorithms can be made noise-free *and* less biased, but raise their own objections.
- **Decision move:** Weigh noise reduction against these competing values explicitly. Choose rules vs standards by asking which produces fewer mistakes and which is more burdensome to build/operate. Don't pursue zero noise reflexively — but recognize today's noise level is usually far too high.
- **Applies / fails when:** Use as the closing sanity check on any noise-reduction program. The bias toward standards over rules often stems from simply ignoring noise — correct for that, but stop short of dehumanizing rigidity.
- **Example:** A social network banning specific words eliminates moderation noise but creates many context-blind errors — "the cure might be worse than the disease."
