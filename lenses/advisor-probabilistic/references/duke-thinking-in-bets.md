# Thinking in Bets — Annie Duke  ·  prefix [TIB]
> Every decision is a bet on an uncertain future: its quality is the process, not the result — separate skill from luck, hold beliefs as probabilities, and recruit others and your future self to fight bias. Dominant lens when an outcome looks "obviously" good or bad in hindsight, when uncertainty and hidden information are high, and when you must judge a decision before (or apart from) knowing how it turned out.

## Principles

### [TIB:01] Decision quality ≠ outcome quality  (ch 1)
- **Essence:** "Resulting" is judging a decision by how it turned out. Outcome = decision quality + luck; a sound process can yield a bad result, and a reckless one a good result (drunk driver who gets home safely). Hindsight bias ("I should have known") rides along with resulting.
- **Decision move:** Evaluate the decision against what was knowable at the time, not against the outcome. When reviewing a loss, ask "was the process bad, or was this a low-probability branch of a good bet?"
- **Applies / fails when:** Applies under uncertainty/luck. Less relevant where outcomes correlate tightly with decisions (near-certain, low-luck domains).
- **Example:** Pete Carroll's Super Bowl pass call had a ~2% interception rate; condemned only because it failed. The CEO who fired a competent president after a thorough process — bad result, reasonable decision.

### [TIB:02] Life is poker, not chess  (ch 1)
- **Essence:** Chess has no hidden info and almost no luck — outcomes reliably signal skill. Real-life decisions are poker: incomplete information, luck, and decisions under uncertainty over time. Treating life like chess makes you read every result as proof of decision quality.
- **Decision move:** Identify how much luck and hidden information a given decision contains before trusting the outcome as feedback. The noisier the domain, the less any single result tells you.
- **Applies / fails when:** Universal framing; the chess-like exception is rare (transparent, deterministic problems).
- **Example:** A novice can beat a poker world champion in one hand; never in one game of chess.

### [TIB:03] Embrace "I'm not sure"  (ch 1)
- **Essence:** "I'm not sure" is often the most accurate answer, not a failure. Good decision-makers don't seek certainty; they estimate how unsure they are. Refusing uncertainty forces black-and-white thinking (a scale with only two notches).
- **Decision move:** Replace yes/no certainty with a best-guess probability and a range of plausible alternatives. Make peace with acting while unsure.
- **Applies / fails when:** Especially valuable for predictions and judgments with limited data; the goal is to *try* for objectivity, not achieve it.
- **Example:** Asked "how likely is 4 heads in a row?" the correct answer is "I'm not sure" — you don't know if the coin is fair or the flipper a magician. Four flips reveal almost nothing.

### [TIB:04] Redefine wrong (and right)  (ch 1)
- **Essence:** A probabilistic prediction isn't wrong merely because the most likely outcome didn't occur — long shots hit. If you aren't wrong when a good bet loses, you also aren't right when a bad bet wins. Worth the trade: losses hurt ~2x as much as wins feel good (loss aversion).
- **Decision move:** Stop using single outcomes as proof of being right/wrong. Live in the continuum of grey, not at the right/wrong extremes.
- **Applies / fails when:** Applies wherever a decision had a known/estimable probability < 100%. Doesn't excuse genuinely bad process.
- **Example:** Brexit and Trump 2016 were called "wrong" by bookmakers/pollsters, but a 30–40% event happens a lot; the odds weren't proven inaccurate by one result.

### [TIB:05] All decisions are bets  (ch 2)
- **Essence:** A bet is "a choice made by thinking about what will probably happen," risking something on a belief. Every decision commits resources to one possible future and forecloses the rejected alternatives — so most bets are against the other versions of yourself you didn't choose. Opportunity cost is always present.
- **Decision move:** Frame any choice (job, parenting, investment, ordering dinner) as a bet: inventory the alternatives, their probabilities, the resources at risk, and the opportunity cost of the paths not taken.
- **Applies / fails when:** Universal; the betting elements are just more visible in some domains (investing) than others.
- **Example:** John Hennigan's $30,000 wager to live in Des Moines was structurally identical to any relocation/job decision — alternatives, consequences, probabilities.

### [TIB:06] Beliefs are formed sloppily, defended stubbornly  (ch 2)
- **Essence:** We don't hear→vet→believe; we hear→believe→(rarely) vet. Default is to accept what we hear as true (Gilbert). Once lodged, beliefs resist correction via motivated reasoning — we reinterpret new data to fit the belief. Being smart makes it worse (bias blind spot grows with cognitive sophistication; numerate people spin data better).
- **Decision move:** Treat your own beliefs as untested until you've actually checked their source and evidence. Assume intelligence won't protect you — install external checks instead.
- **Applies / fails when:** Applies to abstract/secondhand beliefs especially; strongest where the belief flatters identity.
- **Example:** Widely "known" myths (baldness from maternal grandfather, dog-years ×7) never vetted. "They Saw a Game" — fans of opposing teams literally counted different penalties watching the same film.

### [TIB:07] "Wanna bet?" stress-tests beliefs  (ch 2)
- **Essence:** Being challenged to bet money signals someone thinks you're wrong, triggering the rarely-reached vetting step: How do I know this? Source quality? What am I missing? What are the alternatives?
- **Decision move:** Mentally pose "Wanna bet?" to your own confident assertions to force an evidence inventory — even without a literal wager.
- **Applies / fails when:** Internal use is universal; literal challenges damage relationships outside a consenting truthseeking context.
- **Example:** "Citizen Kane obviously won Best Picture." → "Wanna bet?" → sudden, healthy uncertainty (it did not win).

### [TIB:08] State confidence as a probability  (ch 2)
- **Essence:** Replace "I'm confident / not confident" with *how* confident — a 0–100% number or a range of plausible alternatives. This surfaces the probabilistic nature of beliefs, enables small calibrations ("I was 58%, now 46%") instead of painful full reversals, makes you a more credible communicator, and invites others to contribute.
- **Decision move:** Attach a confidence percentage (or range) to every meaningful belief and prediction; update incrementally as evidence arrives.
- **Applies / fails when:** Always useful; the range widens with less knowledge or more luck. Mirrors science's p-values and confidence intervals.
- **Example:** "I'm 60% that Citizen Kane won Best Picture" — admits the 40% the world contradicts you, and signals truthseeking that makes you more believable.

### [TIB:09] Fielding outcomes into the luck vs. skill bucket  (ch 3)
- **Essence:** As the future unfolds, each outcome forces a bet: was this driven by my decisions (skill — learn from it) or by things outside my control (luck — ignore it)? Mis-fielding stops learning. Outcomes are rarely 100% either; ambiguity makes fielding hard.
- **Decision move:** Treat outcome-attribution itself as a high-stakes bet. For each result ask which bucket — and resist the easy answer.
- **Applies / fails when:** Core to learning from experience; hardest where feedback is delayed or noisy.
- **Example:** Nick the Greek fielded seven-deuce wins as "brilliant strategy" and losses as bad luck — never updated, went broke. The SnackWell's eaters couldn't tell if weight gain was their food choice (skill) or metabolism (luck).

### [TIB:10] Self-serving bias corrupts learning  (ch 3)
- **Essence:** Fielding errors are patterned, not random: we credit our wins to skill and blame our losses on luck (and flip it for others — their wins are luck, their losses their fault). Driven by ego/positive self-narrative and all-or-nothing thinking. This is the single biggest barrier to learning from results.
- **Decision move:** Counter via (a) perspective-taking — "if this outcome happened to someone else, how would I field it?" and "if this had happened to me?"; (b) habit substitution — keep the reward (feeling good) but change the routine: get the ego boost from being the best credit-giver and mistake-admitter (Phil Ivey model).
- **Applies / fails when:** Universal and robust; recedes only with deliberate retraining.
- **Example:** Phil Hellmuth: "If it weren't for luck, I'd win every one." Bartman blamed by 40,000 fans for an outcome mostly outside his control while a fan beside him took credit for not touching the ball.

### [TIB:11] Build a truthseeking pod (the buddy system)  (ch 4)
- **Essence:** You can't de-bias alone; others spot your blind spots. But groups can amplify bias (confirmatory thought / echo chambers) unless explicitly chartered for exploratory thought. A productive charter: (1) focus on **accuracy** over confirmation; (2) **accountability**, known in advance; (3) openness to a **diversity** of viewpoints. Three members suffice (two to disagree, one to referee).
- **Decision move:** Recruit a small group and set an explicit charter modifying the normal social contract — reward credit-giving, mistake-admitting, and dissent. Hold members accountable in advance; pre-commit (e.g., loss limits) knowing you'll answer to them.
- **Applies / fails when:** Works only with consenting members; don't impose truthseeking on those who didn't opt in (the "Lettermanning" failure). Beware confirmatory drift — even judges and scientists cluster into clones.
- **Example:** Duke's poker pod (Seidel, brother Howard) required she discuss only her own decisions, not bad luck. Federal appellate panels: a single opposite-party judge has "a large disciplining effect."

### [TIB:12] Mertonian CUDOS — rules of engagement  (ch 5)
- **Essence:** Robert Merton's norms make a group truthseeking: **C**ommunism (share all data — "more is more"; the detail you want to omit is the one to share), **U**niversalism (judge an idea on merit, not its source — "don't shoot the message"), **D**isinterestedness (guard against conflicts of interest, including ego and knowing the outcome), **O**rganized **S**kepticism (cooperative, uncertainty-framed dissent, not confrontation).
- **Decision move:** Be a data-sharer; volunteer uncomfortable details. Disentangle message from messenger (imagine it from a source you love/hate). Express dissent as "I'm not sure about that" / "Have you considered…". Lead with assent and "yes, and" rather than "but" when engaging outside the pod.
- **Applies / fails when:** Designed for chartered groups; the communication tactics (express uncertainty, lead with assent, focus on the future) extend to anyone, including kids.
- **Example:** Duke's exercise: when tempted to dismiss a "bad" player, force yourself to find something they did well — surfaced profitable strategies she'd have missed.

### [TIB:13] Outcome-blind evaluation  (ch 5)
- **Essence:** Knowing how something turned out biases assessment of the decision (a built-in conflict of interest → resulting). Disinterestedness is enforced by hiding the outcome — even physicists use outcome-blind analysis to prevent unconsciously confirming a hypothesis.
- **Decision move:** Deconstruct decisions *before* the outcome is known where possible (trial strategy before the verdict, sales process before the close). When seeking advice after the fact, describe the situation up to the decision point and withhold the result — and withhold your own belief, which is equally contagious.
- **Applies / fails when:** Highly effective for decision review; impractical only when outcome and decision are simultaneous (then omit the result when workshopping).
- **Example:** Duke's poker seminars: she'd describe a hand only to the decision point and answer "How did it turn out?" with "It doesn't matter."

### [TIB:14] Recruit past and future selves (mental time travel)  (ch 6)
- **Essence:** In-the-moment decisions are impulsive (Night Jerry always screws Morning Jerry — temporal discounting). Engaging past and future selves activates the prefrontal/deliberative system. Don't be a "ticker watcher" of your own life: the zoom lens both magnifies and *distorts* (path-dependent emotions — happy losing $100, sad winning $100). Tilt = emotional outcomes hijacking the next decision.
- **Decision move:** Run 10-10-10 — consequences in 10 minutes / 10 months / 10 years (or "how would I feel if past-me had made this?"). Recognize tilt's verbal/physical signs and pre-commit to walk away; "it's all just one long game."
- **Applies / fails when:** Most valuable under emotional intensity or when present-self and future-self interests diverge (savings, sleep, anger). Doesn't make you fully rational — just more often so.
- **Example:** Age-progression retirement apps: subjects shown their aged face allocated ~$178 to retirement vs. ~$74 for those shown their current self.

### [TIB:15] Ulysses contracts and decision-interrupts  (ch 6)
- **Essence:** Past-you binds future-you against predictable irrationality (Odysseus tied to the mast). Contracts can raise barriers (don't go to the food court) or lower them (carry healthy snacks). Even a non-tamper-proof pre-commitment creates a stop-and-think "decision-interrupt."
- **Decision move:** Anticipate your hazard points and pre-commit: automatic retirement contributions, max-bid before house-hunting, settlement floor before negotiating, a "decision swear jar" of red-flag words (certainty language, "wrong," luck/credit phrases) that trigger reflection.
- **Applies / fails when:** Works for foreseeable lapses; binding need not be absolute — the interrupt is the point.
- **Example:** A ride-share booked in advance takes the car keys out of drunk-future-you's hands; loss limits in poker enforced by accountability to the pod.

### [TIB:16] Scenario planning, backcasting, and premortems  (ch 6)
- **Essence:** A decision is a bet on a future, so map the futures first. Enumerate possible outcomes and assign probabilities (if you're worried about guessing, you're already guessing). **Backcasting** imagines success and works backward (the cheerleader); **premortems** imagine failure and work backward (the heckler). Negative visualization ("mental contrasting," Oettingen) makes goals *more* likely, not less. Prospective hindsight raises correct reason-identification ~30%.
- **Decision move:** Build a decision tree: list scenarios, estimate probabilities (even wide ranges beat 0/100%), use expected value (a $100k grant at 25% is worth $25k). Then backcast from "We succeeded!" and premortem from "We failed!" Pre-commit responses to the branches.
- **Applies / fails when:** Improves any strategic choice; premortems also surface dissent safely. Backcasting alone overrepresents the positive space.
- **Example:** After-School All-Stars reprioritized grants by expected value (the $50k grant at 70% beats the $200k at 10%). Carroll's pass gave Seattle three plays vs. two — visible only by scouting the tree.

### [TIB:17] Beware the hindsight-bias chainsaw  (ch 6)
- **Essence:** Time is a tree: one trunk (past), many branches (possible futures), the present where they meet. When one future occurs, we saw off all the other branches and treat what happened as inevitable ("I should have known," "I told you so"). This destroys probabilistic thinking and breeds unproductive regret.
- **Decision move:** Memorialize the scenario tree and probabilities *before* the outcome, then refuse to re-edit it in hindsight. Reattach the severed branches when reviewing — judge the decision against the full set of futures that were live at the time.
- **Applies / fails when:** Critical for fair post-hoc evaluation (legal verdicts, election post-mortems, your own regrets); the antidote is documentation done in advance.
- **Example:** Judge Easterbrook overturned punitive damages: "Hindsight bias is not enough to support a verdict" — no foreseeable explosion when the foreman acted. Nate Silver "blamed" for a 30–40% Trump branch becoming the trunk.
