# The Psychology of Human Misjudgment — Charles T. Munger  ·  prefix [PHM]
> Human judgment is wrecked by ~25 evolved, generally-useful mental shortcuts that misfire — and they fire in confluence (lollapalooza) to produce extreme outcomes; the decider's edge is a memorized checklist of the tendencies and their antidotes.
> Use this lens when a decision feels "obvious," emotionally charged, made under stress or by a group, advised by someone who profits from it, or when several pressures point the same way.

## Principles

### [PHM:01] Incentive superpower & incentive-caused bias  (ch 1)
- **Essence:** Incentives drive behavior far more than people admit, and they bend cognition: a decent person, consciously and subconsciously, rationalizes harmful conduct to get the reward ("agency cost"). Even you will underestimate this every year.
- **Decision move:** Get the incentives right by design. When taking advice, especially fear it when it is especially good for the adviser; learn the basics of the adviser's trade; double-check, disbelieve, or replace much of what you're told. Build anti-gaming controls (cash registers, sound accounting, internal audit) and never reward what's easily faked.
- **Applies / fails when:** Applies wherever pay, status, or promotion ride on an outcome (sales commissions, contractors, professional advice). Less load-bearing for genuinely disinterested parties — but those are rarer than they look.
- **Example:** FedEx fixed its night-shift bottleneck only by paying per shift (go home when done) instead of per hour; the Xerox salesmen pushed the inferior machine because the commission rewarded it.

### [PHM:02] Man-with-a-hammer tendency & the latticework of models  (ch 1, 19)
- **Essence:** "To a man with only a hammer, every problem looks like a nail." Mastering one model makes you force it onto everything (Skinner explaining all of psychology by incentives). The antidote is a broad latticework of mental models from many disciplines, kept fluent by use.
- **Decision move:** Diagnose any important problem against multiple models from different fields, not your favorite one. Practice rarely-used skills (use-it-or-lose-it) so the toolkit stays available; assemble and routinely run a checklist.
- **Applies / fails when:** Strongest risk for experts and specialists analyzing problems that cross disciplinary lines (real-world problems usually do). Less relevant for narrow, well-bounded tasks.
- **Example:** B.F. Skinner ruined his reputation by trying to explain a human utopia purely through incentive conditioning.

### [PHM:03] Liking/loving tendency  (ch 2)
- **Essence:** People are wired to like, love, and seek approval; affection then makes you ignore the loved object's faults, favor anything merely associated with it, and distort facts to protect the bond — amplified by an admiration feedback loop.
- **Decision move:** When you like or admire a person, product, or idea, deliberately re-examine its faults and the facts you may be warping. "See it like it is and love anyway."
- **Applies / fails when:** Applies to hiring, partnerships, marriages, loyalty to mentors/brands. Loving admirable people/ideas is also a genuine life advantage — the danger is only the distortion, not the affection.
- **Example:** A jury disbelieved an admirable woman's false testimony once shown that love for her husband had warped her account.

### [PHM:04] Disliking/hating tendency  (ch 3)
- **Essence:** The obverse of liking: people are born to dislike and hate, which makes them ignore virtues in the disliked object, reject anything associated with it, and distort facts to justify the hatred.
- **Decision move:** Toward rivals you dislike, deliberately upgrade your estimate of their competence and morals; never let dislike set your appraisal subconsciously.
- **Applies / fails when:** Applies in competition, litigation, politics, and tribal/group conflict. Channeling hatred into nonlethal forms (elections, courts) is the civilizational fix.
- **Example:** In business Munger constantly sees people under-rate disliked competitors — a dangerous, usually subconscious error.

### [PHM:05] Doubt-avoidance tendency  (ch 4)
- **Essence:** The brain rushes to remove doubt by reaching a quick decision — useful for prey under threat, harmful for complex modern choices. Triggered chiefly by puzzlement plus stress.
- **Decision move:** On important decisions, force delay before concluding; adopt a "mask" of objectivity (as courts force judges/juries to) so a snap answer isn't locked in.
- **Applies / fails when:** Most dangerous when you are confused and stressed simultaneously. Fast decisions are fine when the cost of delay exceeds the cost of error.
- **Example:** Much of religious faith and many premature commitments are powered by the urge to end discomforting doubt.

### [PHM:06] Inconsistency-avoidance tendency (status-quo & commitment bias)  (ch 5)
- **Essence:** The mind conserves "programming space" by resisting change, so early conclusions, habits, identities, and commitments calcify ("the chains of habit too light to be felt until too strong to be broken"). Combined with doubt-avoidance, this produces first-conclusion bias and confirmation bias; even pretending or making a sacrifice for a position deepens commitment to it.
- **Decision move:** Practice Darwin's habit: actively hunt disconfirming evidence, hardest against your favorite hypotheses. Prevent bad habits/conclusions rather than try to cure them; force yourself (or the group) to hear strong counterarguments before deciding.
- **Applies / fails when:** Universal; especially strong after public commitment, costly initiation, or long-held identity. Beneficial side: keeps people loyal to good roles and oaths.
- **Example:** Franklin won a rival's trust by getting him to lend a book — the lender then had to like Franklin to stay consistent; the same mechanism powered Chinese POW brainwashing.

### [PHM:07] Curiosity tendency  (ch 6)
- **Essence:** Humans are intensely curious, and culture amplifies it; curiosity, sharpened by good education, helps prevent or reduce damage from the other tendencies and yields lifelong wisdom and fun.
- **Decision move:** Treat curiosity as an active discipline — keep digging into how things really work; it is the engine that lets you spot the other biases operating.
- **Applies / fails when:** Always an asset for judgment. The only failure mode is letting it stay idle.
- **Example:** Curiosity-driven Athens produced math and science; "practical" Rome, focused only on engineering, produced almost none.

### [PHM:08] Kantian fairness tendency  (ch 7)
- **Essence:** Acculturated humans follow and expect fair, golden-rule behavior (taking turns, queuing, sharing windfalls); when expected fairness is denied, intense reactive hostility follows.
- **Decision move:** Anticipate that perceived unfairness — not just material loss — will trigger strong backlash; design splits, queues, and rules to be visibly fair to defuse it.
- **Applies / fails when:** Applies to negotiations, compensation design, communities, public processes. Note fairness norms are partly cultural, not universal.
- **Example:** Drivers voluntarily let others merge and strangers line up first-come-first-served absent any signs or enforcement.

### [PHM:09] Envy/jealousy tendency  (ch 8)
- **Essence:** Deeply evolved (the drive to grab scarce food another holds), envy is extreme in myth, religion, and modern institutions yet under-discussed because naming it insults the target. "It is not greed that drives the world but envy" (Buffett).
- **Decision move:** Diagnose disputes for envy as a hidden cause; reduce it structurally (e.g., equal pay among peers where it prevents disorder) rather than hoping reason alone resolves it.
- **Applies / fails when:** Strong in pay-comparison settings (universities, law firms, banks) and among siblings. The taboo on naming it means it's routinely the unspoken real driver.
- **Example:** Many big law firms pay all senior partners alike regardless of contribution, specifically to forestall envy-driven disorder.

### [PHM:10] Reciprocation tendency  (ch 9)
- **Essence:** Humans automatically repay favors and disfavors, largely subconsciously. It facilitates trade, marriage, and cooperation, but lets manipulators extract outsized compliance with small favors or with a retreat from a large request to a smaller one (concession reciprocation).
- **Decision move:** Block the mechanism where it can be exploited: Sam Walton barred purchasing agents from accepting even a hot dog from a vendor. Defer reciprocated hostility — "you can always tell the man off tomorrow."
- **Applies / fails when:** Most dangerous when you spend someone else's money (no personal cost to brake you) and when a favor or concession precedes the ask. Net effect on civilization is strongly positive.
- **Example:** Cialdini tripled compliance (1-in-6 → 1-in-2) by first making an absurd request, then "conceding" to the smaller real one; reciprocated concession also helped greenlight the Watergate burglary.

### [PHM:11] Influence-from-mere-association tendency  (ch 10)
- **Essence:** Mere association — not actual reward — conditions responses: high price read as high quality, products linked to pretty images or past success, and bad news hated because the messenger is associated with it (Persian Messenger syndrome). Past success is dangerously associated with whatever accidentally accompanied it.
- **Decision move:** When repeating a past success, isolate the accidental, non-causal factors and scan for new dangers absent before. Reject stereotyped class-averages as predictors of a specific case. Actively welcome bad news ("only the good news can wait") so messengers aren't punished.
- **Applies / fails when:** Applies to pricing, advertising, gambling streaks, stereotyping, and organizational truth-telling. Fails as a guide whenever the correlation was coincidental.
- **Example:** Napoleon and Hitler invaded Russia after successes elsewhere; CBS's Paley, hostile to bad news, lived "in a cocoon of unreality" and made deal after bad deal.

### [PHM:12] Simple, pain-avoiding psychological denial  (ch 11)
- **Essence:** When reality is too painful, the mind distorts facts until they become bearable. Most extreme around love, death, and chemical dependency.
- **Decision move:** Recognize denial in yourself and others around catastrophic facts; for addiction risk specifically, avoid any conduct likely to drift toward dependency since even a small chance of so great a harm isn't worth it.
- **Applies / fails when:** Applies to grief, addiction, failing ventures one can't admit are failing. Compassionate use (easing the dying) draws no criticism.
- **Example:** A friend's mother refused to believe her son was dead after his plane vanished over the Atlantic.

### [PHM:13] Excessive self-regard tendency & the endowment effect  (ch 12)
- **Essence:** People overrate themselves (90% of Swedish drivers "above average"), their possessions (endowment effect), their loved ones, and especially their own conclusions and self-picked choices. Includes the Tolstoy effect: criminals/under-performers excuse fixable failures instead of fixing them.
- **Decision move:** Underweigh face-to-face impressions and overweigh the candidate's track record in hiring. Force objectivity about yourself, your people, and your own decisions. Institutionally: demanding meritocratic culture plus severance of the worst excuse-makers; personally, fix fixable poor performance rather than excuse it.
- **Applies / fails when:** Applies to hiring, self-assessment, gambling on one's own picks, valuing one's own work. Justified pride (in trustworthiness, in work well done) is constructive — the error is the excess.
- **Example:** Munger had a search committee skip further interviews and appoint the strongest record, citing research on the poor predictive value of interview impressions; he judged the articulate Carly Fiorina hire a likely error of over-weighting presence.

### [PHM:14] Overoptimism tendency  (ch 13)
- **Essence:** "What a man wishes, that also will he believe" — people are excessively optimistic even when doing fine and no threat exists; evolved risk heuristics are inadequate.
- **Decision move:** Replace gut optimism with trained, habitual probability math (Fermat/Pascal) on the actual odds.
- **Applies / fails when:** Applies to forecasts, business plans, gambling. Present even absent pain — it's a baseline distortion, not just a coping response.
- **Example:** Happy people buying lottery tickets at terrible odds.

### [PHM:15] Deprival-superreaction tendency (loss aversion + near-miss)  (ch 14)
- **Essence:** A loss hurts far more than an equal gain helps, and a narrowly missed gain stings as if already owned and seized. Causes misframing (raging over a small loss while ignoring the large stake), bureaucratic turf wars, ideological intolerance of dissent (loss of belief-system support), gambler's "get even" compulsion, and overbidding at auctions.
- **Decision move:** Reframe to compare what actually matters, not what's near. Don't go to open-outcry auctions (Buffett rule). Don't pour good assets into rescuing a failing venture (poker teaches folding). Defuse groupthink with enforced courtesy and deliberately imported able dissenters.
- **Applies / fails when:** Applies to negotiations with "takeaways," labor relations, escalation of commitment, ideology, gambling. Note that loss aversion can occasionally be the rational response too — the error is the irrational intensity.
- **Example:** Munger declined 1,500 more shares of underpriced Belridge Oil because it required selling/borrowing; the stock later sold to Shell at ~$3,700 vs his $115 entry — a ~$5.4M cost of one deprival-driven flinch.

### [PHM:16] Social-proof tendency  (ch 15)
- **Essence:** People automatically think and do what they see others around them doing — and also infer from others' inaction that inaction is correct. Triggered most readily under puzzlement and stress. Both good and bad behavior are contagious.
- **Decision move:** Learn to ignore the examples of others when they are wrong — "few skills are more worth having." Stop bad behavior before it spreads; for kids, manage the quality of their peers rather than exhort. Beware copying competitors' fads.
- **Applies / fails when:** Strongest under isolation + stress + fatigue (the cult/high-pressure-sales recipe) and in crowds/boards. Following the crowd is genuinely efficient when others actually know (which way to the stadium).
- **Example:** Bystander inaction contributed to Kitty Genovese's death; oil companies copied each other into bad mine/fertilizer buying fads; Serpico syndrome = corruption spread by social proof plus incentives.

### [PHM:17] Contrast-misreaction tendency  (ch 16)
- **Essence:** The nervous system registers contrast, not absolute units, so judgment is anchored by comparison — a $1,000 dashboard seems cheap beside a $65,000 car; a series of tiny degradations (the "boiling frog") slides you past disaster because each step contrasts little with the last.
- **Decision move:** Evaluate against true absolute standards and the real baseline, not the adjacent comparison. Watch for small consecutive steps toward disaster — "a small leak will sink a great ship."
- **Applies / fails when:** Applies to pricing/anchoring sales tactics, relationship/standard "downgrades by comparison," and slow-creep risks. Awareness of the ploy is only a partial defense.
- **Example:** A realtor shows three awful overpriced houses first so a merely-bad overpriced one closes easily.

### [PHM:18] Stress-influence tendency  (ch 17)
- **Essence:** Light stress can slightly improve performance; heavy stress causes dysfunction, depression, and even sudden cognition reversals (Pavlov: any dog could be broken down, and breakdowns reversed only by reimposing stress).
- **Decision move:** Avoid making important judgments under heavy stress; recognize that acute-stress depression is among the more reversible ailments and that extreme stress can flip loyalties and beliefs (relevant to cults/coercion).
- **Applies / fails when:** Applies to high-pressure decisions, crises, manipulation settings. Mild pressure (e.g., before an exam) is fine to helpful.
- **Example:** Pavlov's Leningrad-flood dogs, stressed to near-drowning, reversed their conditioned behaviors — a loving dog now hated its trainer.

### [PHM:19] Availability-misweighing tendency  (ch 18)
- **Essence:** The limited-capacity brain works with what's easily recalled and overweighs vivid, available information; it can't use what it can't remember or is blocked from recognizing. "An idea or fact is not worth more merely because it is easily available."
- **Decision move:** Use checklists; deliberately overweigh important factors that don't generate easy numbers and underweigh extra-vivid evidence. Recruit articulate skeptics to argue the opposite of the incumbent view. Exploit vividness constructively for memory and persuasion.
- **Applies / fails when:** Applies whenever salient/recent data crowds out important-but-quiet data (risk estimation, metrics-driven decisions). 
- **Example:** Decision-makers drift to factors that "produce reams of easily available numbers" while ignoring decisive factors that don't.

### [PHM:20] Authority-misinfluence tendency  (ch 22)
- **Essence:** Living in dominance hierarchies, man is built to follow leaders — sometimes into obvious disaster when the leader is wrong or misunderstood.
- **Decision move:** Train people to disobey clearly foolish orders (as copilot simulators do). Be very careful whom you appoint to power, since a bad dominant figure is hard to remove precisely because of this tendency; as a leader, communicate clearly to avoid misread "orders."
- **Applies / fails when:** Applies in cockpits, medicine, corporations, militaries, any hierarchy. Even trained subordinates still over-comply under live authority pressure.
- **Example:** Milgram's subjects delivered what they believed were torturous shocks on a professor's say-so; a nurse put eardrops in a patient's anus reading "r. ear"; a copilot crashed misreading the general's body shift as a command.

### [PHM:21] Twaddle tendency  (ch 23)
- **Essence:** Humans, gifted with language, pour out chatter that damages serious work; some produce copious twaddle, like a honeybee doing an incoherent dance when it can't encode a task.
- **Decision move:** As an administrator, keep the prattling non-contributors away from the people doing the real work.
- **Applies / fails when:** Applies in any organization where talk substitutes for or interferes with substantive work.
- **Example:** "The principal job of an academic administration is to keep the people who don't matter from interfering with the work of the people who do."

### [PHM:22] Reason-respecting tendency  (ch 24)
- **Essence:** People love accurate cognition and learn far better when told *why*; "Why?" is the Rosetta stone of mental life. But the tendency is so strong that even meaningless or false reasons boost compliance.
- **Decision move:** Always supply genuine reasons with orders and lessons (Carl Braun: state who/what/where/when/why or be fired). Stay alert that manipulators exploit this with claptrap "because" reasons.
- **Applies / fails when:** Applies to teaching, management, persuasion. The dark side: a hollow "because…" still increases compliance.
- **Example:** Experimental subjects let line-cutters ahead at the copier when given the empty reason "because I have to make some copies."

### [PHM:23] Lollapalooza tendency  (ch 25)
- **Essence:** The master principle: when several psychological tendencies all push toward the same outcome at once, they don't merely add — they combine to produce extreme, often catastrophic or cult-like results ("snapping").
- **Decision move:** In any high-stakes situation, run the full tendency list as a checklist and ask which ones are acting *together* and in which direction — confluence is where the real danger and the extreme outcomes live.
- **Applies / fails when:** Applies precisely to the situations textbooks miss because they study one tendency at a time. The whole essay exists to make this analysis possible.
- **Example:** The McDonnell Douglas evacuation-test disaster was driven by reward-superresponse + doubt-avoidance + authority-misinfluence + inconsistency-avoidance + social-proof + deprival-superreaction acting in confluence; Milgram's extreme result required ~six tendencies at once.
