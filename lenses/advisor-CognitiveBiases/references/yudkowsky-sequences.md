# Rationality: From AI to Zombies — Eliezer Yudkowsky  ·  prefix [SEQ]
> Rationality is the systematic art of forming beliefs that track reality (epistemic) and choosing actions that achieve your values (instrumental) — and the discipline of noticing where a human mind quietly fails at both. Use this lens when a decision turns on what is actually true, when you suspect you are arguing to defend a conclusion you already reached, or when a belief or word feels meaningful but is doing no predictive work.

## Principles

### [SEQ:01] Two Kinds of Rationality — and the Goal Is Winning  (ch A/1)
- **Essence:** Epistemic rationality = systematically making your beliefs more accurate (map matching territory). Instrumental rationality = systematically steering reality toward outcomes you value ("winning"). Both are measured by results, not by ritual or by sounding logical.
- **Decision move:** Separate the question "what is actually true here?" from "what action best achieves my values given that truth?" Judge any technique by whether it produces more accuracy or more winning — never by whether it feels rigorous.
- **Applies / fails when:** Applies whenever a decision depends on a fact of the matter. Fails as a slogan: if a method makes you "more rational" but systematically lose, it isn't rationality — rationalists should win.
- **Example:** Choosing to two-box vs one-box, or to act on a gut model: pick whichever actually yields the better outcome, not whichever fits a stereotype of "being logical."

### [SEQ:02] The Map Is Not the Territory  (ch A/4, sequence O)
- **Essence:** Your beliefs are a map; reality is the territory. Truth is correspondence between the two. Ignorance, probability, and confusion are features of your map, not properties of the world — the world is always definite.
- **Decision move:** When something seems "inherently uncertain" or "mysterious," relocate the uncertainty into your model and ask what observation would update it. Don't treat your own confusion as a fact about reality.
- **Applies / fails when:** Applies to every factual disagreement. Boundary: it does not collapse genuine normative or preference questions into factual ones.
- **Example:** "Is the LHC dangerous?" — the danger is a fixed fact; your 1-in-a-million feeling is a property of your map, to be sharpened by better models, not asserted as the territory's odds.

### [SEQ:03] Make Your Beliefs Pay Rent (in Anticipated Experience)  (ch B/11)
- **Essence:** Every meaningful belief must constrain what you expect to see — ideally forbidding some experiences. Beliefs connected only to other beliefs ("floating beliefs") feel like knowledge but predict nothing.
- **Decision move:** For any belief driving a decision, ask: "What do I expect to observe because this is true? What must NOT happen if it's true?" If you can't answer, the belief is doing no work — evict it from the decision.
- **Applies / fails when:** Applies to factual claims. Distinguish from values/preferences, which legitimately needn't predict sensory experience.
- **Example:** "This vendor is reliable" pays rent if it predicts on-time delivery you'd bet on; "our culture is innovative" that survives any outcome is a floating belief.

### [SEQ:04] Mysterious Answers Explain Nothing  (ch D/35; also D/30,34,36)
- **Essence:** A real explanation makes some outcomes more likely than others. Pseudo-explanations (phlogiston, élan vital, "emergence," "complexity") fit every outcome equally and merely re-label the mystery as a substance — curiosity-stoppers that feel satisfying while adding zero predictive power.
- **Decision move:** Test any proposed cause by asking what it forbids. If it would "explain" the opposite result just as easily, treat it as a semantic stopsign and keep investigating.
- **Applies / fails when:** Applies to causal claims and root-cause analyses. Boundary: genuinely simple true causes can look "obvious" — the test is predictive constraint, not complexity.
- **Example:** "The project failed because of poor communication" explains every project outcome; it stops inquiry without naming a fixable mechanism.

### [SEQ:05] Conservation of Expected Evidence  (ch C/28)
- **Essence:** For every expectation of evidence there is an equal and opposite expectation of counter-evidence. You cannot expect — on average — to be more confident after an observation, whatever it turns out to be. If a result would confirm your belief, the opposite result must disconfirm it.
- **Decision move:** Before running a test or seeking data, ask both "what would confirm?" and "what would disconfirm, and by how much?" If no result could lower your confidence, the test is rigged and uninformative.
- **Applies / fails when:** A law of probability; always holds. Violated in practice by tests designed so every outcome is spun as supporting the favored view.
- **Example:** "If the candidate is calm, they're confident; if nervous, they care deeply" — heads-I-win-tails-I-win means the interview gave you no real evidence.

### [SEQ:06] Absence of Evidence Is Evidence of Absence  (ch C/27)
- **Essence:** If observing X would raise the probability of a hypothesis, then failing to observe X must lower it (the symmetric consequence of [SEQ:05]). The strength of the downward update depends on how reliably the hypothesis would have produced X.
- **Decision move:** Treat "we found nothing" as a real, quantifiable update — not a null. Ask: if the thing were true, how likely were we to have seen a sign by now? The more likely, the stronger the disconfirmation.
- **Applies / fails when:** Strong when detection was likely; weak when the hypothesis predicts little visible evidence anyway (then absence barely updates).
- **Example:** No customer complaints about a feature is mild evidence it's fine — strong only if unhappy users reliably complain.

### [SEQ:07] Notice Your Confusion  (ch C/26, "Your Strength as a Rationalist")
- **Essence:** Reality is never actually inconsistent; a feeling that a story "doesn't quite add up" is data — your model is wrong somewhere. The skill is to register that flicker of confusion instead of explaining it away.
- **Decision move:** When a fact, explanation, or excuse feels slightly off, stop and treat the confusion as a flag. Ask what model would make it un-surprising — often the honest answer is "the claim is false."
- **Applies / fails when:** Applies to evaluating reports, alibis, dashboards, narratives. Fails if you suppress the signal to keep social peace or a cherished belief.
- **Example:** A teammate's explanation of a metric anomaly leaves you vaguely uneasy — that unease, not your post-hoc rationalization, is the signal to dig.

### [SEQ:08] The Bottom Line  (ch G/70)
- **Essence:** The conclusion you'll defend is determined the moment you "write the bottom line." Whatever clever arguments you append afterward carry no evidential weight about reality — they only reveal what you already decided. The rationality of a belief is fixed by the process that produced the bottom line, not by the eloquence above it.
- **Decision move:** Ask whether your reasoning came before or after your conclusion. If after, discard the arguments as evidence and re-derive the answer from the actual evidence with the conclusion unknown.
- **Applies / fails when:** Universal diagnostic for motivated reasoning. Boundary: explaining a conclusion to others is fine — just don't mistake post-hoc justification for the reason you should believe it.
- **Example:** You've decided to hire the candidate, then list reasons. Those reasons can't be your evidence — you'd have found reasons either way.

### [SEQ:09] Update Yourself Incrementally  (ch G/68)
- **Essence:** Belief is a dial, not a switch. A correct-but-inexact theory still meets occasional contrary evidence; the move is to shift confidence down a notch and wait, not to defend at all costs or abandon wholesale. Think quantitatively, not black-and-white.
- **Decision move:** On each new datum, nudge your credence up or down proportionally. Permit gentle counter-evidence against beliefs you favor without panic — "down a little" is normal, not a crisis.
- **Applies / fails when:** Applies to all probabilistic beliefs. Note: an *exact* theory that fails is in real trouble — incremental tolerance is for inexact models.
- **Example:** One bad quarter from a strong supplier lowers your confidence slightly; you don't fire them, and you don't pretend it didn't happen.

### [SEQ:10] Is That Your True Rejection?  (ch G/77)
- **Essence:** The objection people voice is frequently not the real driver of their belief. They offer a defensible-sounding reason while the actual cause (status, identity, a different unstated belief) goes unexamined — so refuting the stated objection changes nothing.
- **Decision move:** Before answering an objection, ask: "If I fully resolved this point, would you actually change your mind?" Surface the true rejection; argue that instead. Apply it to yourself first.
- **Applies / fails when:** Applies to negotiation, persuasion, self-audit. Boundary: sometimes the stated reason IS the real one — confirm rather than assume hidden motives.
- **Example:** A stakeholder says "the data isn't strong enough"; the real rejection is the change threatens their team's headcount.

### [SEQ:11] Privileging the Hypothesis / One Argument Against an Army  (ch G/69)
- **Essence:** Merely raising a hypothesis to attention is itself a cost that must be justified by evidence — and re-rehearsing arguments you already counted is double-counting. A single belief, defended by re-citing known support against each new objection, can fend off an army of valid counter-arguments.
- **Decision move:** Notice when a hypothesis got onto your shortlist without evidence locating it. When new counter-evidence arrives, don't replay your old supporting points — integrate the new evidence and shift down.
- **Applies / fails when:** Applies to investigations, suspicions, and shortlists. Boundary: legitimate priors can privilege a hypothesis — the error is privileging with no locating evidence.
- **Example:** Treating one named suspect as guilty and re-listing the same three clues to dismiss each new alibi, instead of weighing the alibis.

### [SEQ:12] Politics Is the Mind-Killer  (ch F/57)
- **Essence:** When an issue is tied to tribal/coalition stakes, reasoning is hijacked by the need to win for one's side: arguments become soldiers, concessions feel like treason, and accuracy collapses. Knowing more often makes it worse (sophistication effect).
- **Decision move:** Strip tribal markers from the decision. Avoid politically charged examples when teaching or analyzing something unrelated. When the topic is inherently political, deliberately hold both sides' best case and watch for "argument-as-soldier" reflexes.
- **Applies / fails when:** Applies wherever identity/coalition is at stake (org politics, culture wars). Fails as an excuse to never engage real political decisions — it's a warning to engage more carefully.
- **Example:** A reorg debate splits into "ours vs theirs" camps; people defend positions by team loyalty, not by which structure actually serves the goal.

### [SEQ:13] Affective Death Spiral / Halo Effect  (ch J/106, 100, 103)
- **Essence:** A strongly positive affect toward one attribute leaks into judgments of all attributes (halo), and admiration can spiral: each good feature is taken as reason the thing must have more good features, with no brake. Affect substitutes for evidence (the affect heuristic).
- **Decision move:** Evaluate each attribute independently and on its own evidence. Install a "brake" — actively seek the downsides of things you admire and the upsides of things you dislike. Beware unbounded enthusiasm with no disconfirming check.
- **Applies / fails when:** Applies to evaluating people, products, ideologies, and your own pet projects. Boundary: correlated competence is real — the error is inferring it from affect rather than evidence.
- **Example:** A charismatic founder is assumed to also have sound finances, good ethics, and a defensible market — each merely radiating from the initial glow.

### [SEQ:14] Leave a Line of Retreat  (ch K/128)
- **Essence:** People resist evaluating whether X is true because the *consequences* of X being true feel unbearable. By first concretely visualizing what you would do, and that life would go on, if X were true, you free yourself to assess X's truth honestly.
- **Decision move:** Before judging a threatening proposition, spend a moment planning your response *assuming it's true*. Make the worst case survivable in imagination, then return to weighing the evidence dispassionately.
- **Applies / fails when:** Applies to emotionally loaded beliefs (a strategy is failing, a relationship is over, you were wrong). Boundary: it eases evaluation — it does not itself decide truth.
- **Example:** Afraid your flagship product is dying, you first sketch the pivot plan; with the catastrophe defanged, you can read the churn data straight.

### [SEQ:15] Crisis of Faith and the Importance of Saying "Oops"  (ch K/121, 129)
- **Essence:** Admitting only small, local errors yields only small, local fixes; a big improvement requires admitting a big, foundational mistake. Genuinely rethinking a deeply held belief demands a deliberate, effortful "crisis of faith" — not the gentle drift the mind prefers.
- **Decision move:** When evidence keeps nibbling at a core assumption, schedule a real reconsideration from scratch (steelman the alternative, recompute as if you'd never committed). Say "I was wrong" cleanly rather than patching symptom by symptom.
- **Applies / fails when:** Applies to sunk-cost-laden strategies and long-held models. Fails if used to thrash on every belief — reserve full crises for load-bearing assumptions.
- **Example:** Enron patched each accounting hole rather than admitting the business model was broken; the missing move was one humbling "this was a mistake from the start."

### [SEQ:16] Hold Off On Proposing Solutions  (ch I/98)
- **Essence:** The instant a solution is named, it anchors the discussion, attracts loyalty, and contaminates further thought — the option space collapses before the problem is understood. Forcing the group to sit in the problem first produces better and more numerous options.
- **Decision move:** Impose a rule: fully characterize the problem and constraints before anyone may propose a fix. Generate the problem-understanding first; let solutions come only after.
- **Applies / fails when:** Applies to design, strategy, and group brainstorming. Boundary: in true emergencies, a fast adequate action beats a delayed optimal one.
- **Example:** A team that defines "what exactly is causing churn and for whom" before anyone says "let's build feature X" finds options the premature-solution path would have foreclosed.

### [SEQ:17] Dissolve the Question, Don't Answer It  (sequence N; ch 16, 34)
- **Essence:** Many hard questions ("does free will exist?", "is this a real X?") are confusing because of a flaw in how the mind represents them, not because reality is conflicted. The fix is to ask why the question *feels* confusing — to dissolve it — rather than to pick a side.
- **Decision move:** When a debate is interminable, step back and diagnose the cognitive algorithm generating the puzzle. Replace "which answer is right?" with "what cognitive process makes this feel like a question?"
- **Applies / fails when:** Applies to definitional/conceptual deadlocks and "wrong questions." Boundary: many questions are genuinely empirical — don't dissolve a question that just needs data.
- **Example:** "Is a hot-dog a sandwich?" dissolves once you see you're disputing category boundaries, not any fact about the food.

### [SEQ:18] Disputing Definitions Is a Trap — Argue Anticipations  (sequence N; ch on 37 ways words can be wrong)
- **Essence:** Arguing over what a word "really means" hides a substantive disagreement (or none at all). Words point to clusters of correlated features in thingspace, not to necessary-and-sufficient essences; an empty definitional fight predicts no difference in experience.
- **Decision move:** When a dispute hinges on a word, "taboo" the word and re-state the claim in concrete anticipations. If both sides expect the same observations, the fight is empty; if not, the real disagreement is now visible.
- **Applies / fails when:** Applies whenever a decision stalls on terminology ("is this a 'bug' or a 'feature'?", "is it a 'recession'?"). Boundary: shared definitions genuinely matter for contracts and coordination — fix them, don't litigate essences.
- **Example:** "Is this launch 'done'?" — taboo "done" and specify which tests must pass and which users must be live; the verbal dispute evaporates.

### [SEQ:19] The Third Alternative  (ch E/47)
- **Essence:** A forced choice between two options — often one obviously bad — is usually a failure to search, frequently sustained because one option serves a hidden comfort (hope, identity). Real life rarely offers only two paths.
- **Decision move:** When boxed into "either A or B," actively hunt for C. Ask what need the false dichotomy is protecting, and whether a third option meets the real goal without the bad trade-off.
- **Applies / fails when:** Applies to ultimatums, "necessary evils," and either/or framings. Boundary: occasionally only two options truly exist — but assume not until you've searched.
- **Example:** "We must either ship buggy now or miss the quarter" ignores the third alternative of cutting scope to ship a smaller solid release on time.

### [SEQ:20] Shut Up and Multiply  (ch W/281-283, scope insensitivity / one life against the world)
- **Essence:** Moral and risk intuitions are scope-insensitive — saving 2,000 birds feels barely different from 200,000, and a "warm glow" tracks effort, not impact. When stakes are quantitative (lives, dollars, risk), explicit expected-value computation must override the feeling. Two lives are twice one life; maximize, don't satisfice.
- **Decision move:** For decisions over numbers of people, money, or probability, do the arithmetic with the "boring zeros" part of your brain. Multiply magnitude by probability; choose the higher expected value even when it lacks emotional vividness.
- **Applies / fails when:** Applies to allocation, triage, altruism, and risk. Boundary: only where outcomes are genuinely commensurable and quantifiable; don't false-multiply incommensurable values or pseudo-numbers (see [SEQ:21]).
- **Example:** Funding a cure for a rare vivid disease (100 people) over a dull common one (10,000 deaths) is the scope-insensitive error; multiply and fund the larger expected lives saved.

### [SEQ:21] When (Not) to Use Probabilities  (ch W/290)
- **Essence:** The laws of probability always govern, but assigning a *made-up* number (one not produced by any numerical method) can be worse than honest verbal uncertainty — it lends fake authority, obscures general consequences, and defeats gut algorithms that may reason better in their native mode.
- **Decision move:** Use explicit probabilities when you have a real numerical foundation; otherwise reason with "likely/unlikely" and debate the general rule rather than dignifying a butt-number as data. Never let a fabricated percentage end a discussion.
- **Applies / fails when:** Applies to high-uncertainty, low-data judgments (tail risks, novel events). Boundary: any genuine numerical grounding beats vague feeling — humans are terrible statisticians; the warning is specifically against *fabricated* precision.
- **Example:** Quoting "0.0001% chance the experiment ends the world" gives shaky reasoning undue authority; better to debate the policy of running experiments under irreducible uncertainty.

### [SEQ:22] Knowing About Biases Can Hurt You  (ch G/67)
- **Essence:** A list of biases is a double-edged tool: people deploy bias-knowledge selectively as ammunition to dismiss conclusions they dislike, while their own favored beliefs feel exempt. Greater intelligence/knowledge can make reasoning *worse* (sophistication effect, disconfirmation bias) by supplying more counter-arguments.
- **Decision move:** Aim every bias concept at *yourself first*. Before accusing another's view of a bias, check whether you'd accept the same standard against your own conclusion. Treat "you're just biased" as a fully-general counterargument to be distrusted.
- **Applies / fails when:** Applies to anyone wielding this very advisor. Boundary: biases are real and worth knowing — the failure mode is asymmetric application, not the knowledge itself.
- **Example:** Learning about overconfidence, someone concludes "experts can't be trusted" — then over-trusts their own contrarian extrapolation, the bias salient only against others.
