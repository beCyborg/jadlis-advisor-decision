# Rationality for Mortals: How People Cope with Uncertainty — Gerd Gigerenzer  ·  prefix [RFM]
> Rationality is not adherence to logic or optimization but the ecological fit between simple heuristics and the structure of the environment; under genuine uncertainty, less information and computation can yield better decisions. Use this lens when a decision happens under uncertainty (not calculable risk), when someone is being judged "irrational" against a formula, or when a "bias" or confusing statistic is steering a choice.

## Principles

### [RFM:01] Ecological rationality  (ch 1)
- **Essence:** A heuristic is neither rational nor irrational in itself — only relative to an environment. Rationality is the match between the mind (one blade of Simon's scissors) and the structure of the task environment (the other blade). You cannot judge a strategy by looking at cognition alone.
- **Decision move:** Before calling a rule good or bad, ask "in what environment does it succeed, and is this that environment?" Specify the environment structure (uncertainty level, number of options, redundancy/variability of cues, sample size) and the success criterion before evaluating.
- **Applies / fails when:** Applies to all judgments under uncertainty. The label "ecologically rational" deliberately does not claim the rule evolved for this or occurs in nature — it is only a performance claim for a specified currency and environment.
- **Example:** Take-the-Best beats multiple regression in noncompensatory environments but loses in compensatory ones; the verdict flips with environment, not with the rule.

### [RFM:02] Reject content-blind norms  (ch 1, 4)
- **Essence:** Using a law of logic/probability (conjunction rule, material conditional, invariance) as the "rational answer" independent of a problem's meaning and goal mistakes human intelligence for error. English "and," "probable," "if-then" are polysemous; people intelligently infer meaning from semantics and pragmatics.
- **Decision move:** When someone is declared irrational for violating a formula, check whether the formula ignored the content and purpose. Re-ask the question so the intended meaning is unambiguous (e.g., "how many of 100…" instead of "more probable") before concluding a real error exists.
- **Applies / fails when:** Applies wherever a verbal/social problem is scored against a purely syntactic rule. Logic is a legitimate norm only in restricted, well-defined domains.
- **Example:** The Linda "conjunction fallacy" largely disappears when "probable" is replaced by "how many," because the apparent error was a reasonable reading of ambiguous words.

### [RFM:03] Many "biases" are environment artifacts, not mind defects  (ch 1, 7)
- **Essence:** Several celebrated cognitive illusions are reflections of the task environment or of researchers' unrepresentative sampling, not stable mental flaws. Every unbiased mind or machine would show the same pattern.
- **Decision move:** Before attributing a failure to a person's flawed mind, check the information environment: was the sample selected or representative? Is the apparent error just regression toward the mean from unsystematic error? Fix the environment/representation before "debiasing" the person.
- **Applies / fails when:** Applies to overconfidence, base-rate "neglect," and similar effects shown to shrink under representative sampling. Does not mean all biases are illusory — it means check the environment first.
- **Example:** Overconfidence in general-knowledge questions vanished when questions were sampled randomly from the world instead of hand-picked for surprising answers (Juslin et al.).

### [RFM:04] The adaptive toolbox  (ch 1, 5)
- **Essence:** The mind is not one general optimizer but a toolbox of specialized, domain-specific heuristics built from a few reusable building blocks (search rule, stopping rule, decision rule). Simon's taxonomy: bounded rationality ⊃ heuristic search ⊃ satisficing.
- **Decision move:** Identify which tool fits the present problem rather than forcing one universal method. Decompose any decision rule into its search/stopping/decision blocks to compare candidates and to teach or design new rules.
- **Applies / fails when:** Applies to modeling and improving real decision processes. The cost: it is a "piecemeal" program — no single grand formula.
- **Example:** Catching a fly ball uses the gaze heuristic; choosing among funds uses 1/N; inferring a larger city uses recognition — different tools, not one calculus.

### [RFM:05] Optimization is frequently impossible or pointless  (ch 1, 5)
- **Essence:** Optimization (computing the provable best) fails when problems are computationally intractable (NP-hard), have multiple incommensurable goals, rest on imprecise/subjective criteria, are unfamiliar with scarce time, or are ill-defined. Optimization is a math process on a simplified model — it does not guarantee an optimal real-world outcome.
- **Decision move:** Don't demand the optimum when no optimum is knowable. Switch from "what is best?" to "what is good enough and robust here?" Beware that adding realistic constraints to keep optimizing often makes the model harder and less realistic, not more.
- **Applies / fails when:** Applies under genuine uncertainty (most consequential life/business choices: whom to marry, what stocks, unfamiliar games). Optimization is fine for well-defined, familiar, single-criterion problems.
- **Example:** Markowitz used the equal-split 1/N rule for his own retirement, not his Nobel-winning optimization model.

### [RFM:06] Fast-and-frugal heuristics exploit evolved capacities + environment  (ch 2)
- **Essence:** A fast (little time) and frugal (little information) heuristic works because it leverages hard-wired/learned abilities (object tracking, recognition, running) and the structure of the world, transforming a complex problem into a simple one. It models the actual process, enabling predictions an as-if optimization model cannot make.
- **Decision move:** Look for the single transforming variable that lets you skip the full causal computation. Build/choose a rule with explicit search-stop-decide blocks that a novice can be taught.
- **Applies / fails when:** Works when an evolved capacity and an exploitable environment structure exist. A heuristic is domain-specific and can fail outside its niche.
- **Example:** The gaze heuristic — keep the angle of gaze to the ball constant — catches fly balls without computing any trajectory, wind, or spin; the same rule (inverted) avoids mid-air collisions for pilots.

### [RFM:07] Recognition heuristic and the less-is-more effect  (ch 2)
- **Essence:** If you recognize one of two objects and not the other, infer the recognized one scores higher on the criterion. When recognition validity exceeds knowledge validity (α > β), more recognition can lower accuracy — a less-is-more effect, occurring across people, across domains, and during learning, and even more strongly in groups.
- **Decision move:** In competitive domains where name recognition correlates with quality, trust recognition as a default — and recognize that an outside, less-informed judge may be more accurate. In groups, let a member's non-recognition legitimately override others' partial knowledge.
- **Applies / fails when:** Works when ignorance is systematic (recognition strongly correlates with criterion). Fails when recognition stems from criterion-irrelevant fame (e.g., Chernobyl) or when you have direct criterion knowledge.
- **Example:** Germans, recognizing fewer U.S. cities, out-guessed Americans on which of Detroit/Milwaukee is larger because Americans recognized both and lost the cue.

### [RFM:08] One-reason decision making — Take-the-Best  (ch 2)
- **Essence:** Search cues in order of validity, stop at the first cue that discriminates between options, and decide on that cue alone — ignoring all remaining cues (noncompensatory/lexicographic). Cue validities are unconditional, which is what makes it simple and robust.
- **Decision move:** Rank your reasons by how often each alone points to the right answer; act on the first decisive reason and ignore the rest. Don't try to weight-and-add everything.
- **Applies / fails when:** Matches or beats weighting-and-adding when cue weights vary greatly (noncompensatory information), information is scarce, or cues are redundant (positively correlated). Loses to tallying when cues have roughly equal weight (compensatory).
- **Example:** British magistrates' bail decisions and GPs' lipid-drug prescriptions are well predicted by one good reason; people adapt toward Take-the-Best when feedback reveals a noncompensatory environment.

### [RFM:09] Robustness beats fit — ignore information to predict well  (ch 2, 5)
- **Essence:** Fitting past data (more parameters → better fit, a mathematical truism) is not predicting new data. Complex models overfit by mistaking noise for signal. Simple heuristics with few/zero free parameters generalize better; "in judgments under uncertainty one must ignore information to make good predictions — the art is to ignore the right kind."
- **Decision move:** Prefer the simpler model/rule when uncertainty is high and data are limited; test predictive accuracy by cross-validation (out-of-sample), never by goodness-of-fit. A great fit may be a warning sign, not a success.
- **Applies / fails when:** Holds under high uncertainty, small samples, and out-of-population transfer. With ample data and stable environments, the gap shrinks and richer models can win.
- **Example:** Take-the-Best and tallying out-predicted multiple regression across 20 real-world datasets even though regression fit the training data best.

### [RFM:10] Fast-and-frugal trees for classification  (ch 2, 3)
- **Essence:** A classification tree with M+1 exits (vs. 2^M for a full tree) asks ordered yes/no questions and allows a decision at every node. Same building blocks as Take-the-Best; transparent, fast, and often more accurate than logistic regression while being trusted and actually used by practitioners.
- **Decision move:** Build the decision as a short sequence of ordered binary checks, each able to terminate the process, rather than a single composite score. Order by validity, ignoring cue dependencies for robustness.
- **Applies / fails when:** Ideal for high-stakes, time-pressured triage where transparency drives adoption and where cue weights vary. Less suited to compensatory environments with equally weighted cues.
- **Example:** The coronary-care-unit allocation tree (Green & Mehr) beat physicians and logistic regression and was still in use years later because doctors understood and trusted it.

### [RFM:11] Good errors are functional — to think is to err  (ch 4)
- **Essence:** Every intelligent system must make errors, because it goes beyond the given information to make inferences under uncertainty. "Good errors" (a child overregularizing verbs, perceptual illusions, mutation in evolution) are the indispensable by-product of intelligence; a system that never erred could not generalize or learn.
- **Decision move:** Distinguish good errors (functional, the cost of learning/generalizing) from blunders (inattention). Don't engineer all error out of a system or a person — preserve the productive risk-taking. Treat an apparent mistake as a possible clue to an adaptive underlying rule.
- **Applies / fails when:** Applies wherever inference goes beyond available data (perception, language learning, prediction). The distinction breaks down only if you wrongly assume the world is certain and fully knowable.
- **Example:** Baseball players "wrongly" run in an arc and can't predict where the ball lands — by-products of the gaze heuristic that nonetheless succeed; the errors need no correction.

### [RFM:12] Benefits of cognitive limits — less can be more  (ch 4)
- **Essence:** Cognitive limitations both constrain and enable. Limited memory acts as a filter that aids abstraction (perfect memory destroys the ability to generalize); a "starting small" memory lets children/networks learn grammar; the 7±2 limit forces small samples that detect correlations faster.
- **Decision move:** Don't assume more memory, more data, more processing is always better. When designing learning or decision systems, consider deliberately restricting input/memory to force focus on what generalizes.
- **Applies / fails when:** Applies to learning, language acquisition, correlation detection. It is a claim about adaptive trade-offs, not a license for ignorance everywhere.
- **Example:** Shereshevsky's limitless memory left him unable to grasp the gist of a story or recognize faces; Elman's neural net learned full grammar only when its memory was first restricted then grown.

### [RFM:13] Dread-risk avoidance can be the larger danger  (ch 6)
- **Essence:** People react to dread risks (low-probability, high-damage events killing many at once) with strong avoidance, while ignoring statistically larger risks spread over time. The avoidance itself — mediated through minds — can cause more harm than the original event ("indirect damage").
- **Decision move:** When a vivid catastrophe triggers avoidance, quantify the risk of the substitute behavior before switching. Communicate comparative base rates so emotional reactions can be recognized and moderated.
- **Applies / fails when:** Applies to terrorism, disasters, screening scares — anywhere salient mass-casualty fear redirects behavior. Note the avoidance is not inevitable: culture and infrastructure modulate it.
- **Example:** After 9/11, Americans drove instead of flying; an estimated ~1,500 extra road deaths in the following year — six times the 256 who died on the four planes. Spaniards after the Madrid bombing did not switch to cars, and saw no such surplus.

### [RFM:14] Specify the reference class — single-event probabilities are ambiguous  (ch 8, 9)
- **Essence:** A single-event probability ("30% chance of rain tomorrow") by definition omits the class of events it refers to, so listeners silently fill in different classes (days, time, region, meteorologists) and reach different meanings. The confusion is in the missing reference class, not in probability per se.
- **Decision move:** Whenever you state or hear a single-event probability, attach the reference class explicitly: "in 3 of 10 days for which I make this forecast…" Convert to a frequency statement, which always carries a class.
- **Applies / fails when:** Critical in weather, medical side-effects, recidivism prediction, DNA testimony — any single-case risk communicated to non-experts. The same number can be true under all interpretations, so the ambiguity is invisible.
- **Example:** A psychiatrist's "30–50% chance of sexual side effects" was read by patients as "in 30–50% of my sexual encounters"; reframing as "3–5 of every 10 patients" removed the alarm.

### [RFM:15] Natural frequencies over conditional probabilities  (ch 9, 12)
- **Essence:** Bayesian inference becomes transparent when information is given as natural frequencies (counts from natural sampling, without normalizing) instead of conditional probabilities. Natural frequencies all refer to the same class, so the base rate is already embedded and the posterior reduces to d/(d+f). Not any frequency works — only natural (non-normalized) ones.
- **Decision move:** Translate any base-rate + sensitivity + false-positive problem into "X of N… of those, Y…" form before reasoning. Teach and demand this representation in medicine, law, counseling.
- **Applies / fails when:** Applies to all elementary Bayesian/diagnostic inference (test result → disease probability). Relative or normalized frequencies do NOT produce the simplification.
- **Example:** Physicians' mammography estimates ranged 1–90% with conditional probabilities; with natural frequencies ~87% gave the correct ~1-in-10. Even Chinese 6th-graders solved Bayesian problems with natural frequencies (impossible for them in probabilities).

### [RFM:16] Absolute risk over relative risk  (ch 9)
- **Essence:** Relative risk reductions ("25% fewer breast-cancer deaths") look impressive but hide the magnitude and refer to a different class (those who die unscreened) than the patient belongs to. The same benefit as an absolute risk (1 in 1,000) or number-needed-to-treat provokes a realistic, much smaller impression.
- **Decision move:** Always demand/convert to absolute risk reduction and NNT. Be alert to mixed framing — benefits in relative terms (big numbers), harms in absolute terms (small numbers) — designed to mislead.
- **Applies / fails when:** Applies to screening, drugs, any treatment-benefit communication and to institutional manipulation of informed consent. For population resource decisions, the population-impact number is the relevant frame.
- **Example:** "25% reduction" from mammography = 4 vs 3 deaths per 1,000 = an absolute reduction of 1 in 1,000; a 100% pill-thrombosis "increase" was 1→2 per 14,000, causing needless abortions when reported as relative.

### [RFM:17] Framing carries real information; invariance is not always normative  (ch 4, 9)
- **Essence:** Logically equivalent statements ("half full"/"half empty," "90% survive"/"10% die") are not pragmatically equivalent — speakers leak surplus information (history, recommendation, what is salient) through the frame, and listeners rightly read between the lines. Treating every framing effect as irrational error is itself a mistake.
- **Decision move:** Read a frame as data about the speaker's intent and reference point, not just the literal number. When you must be neutral, present both frames (positive and negative, gain and loss) to balance the pull.
- **Applies / fails when:** Applies to risk and choice communication. Loss frames push action more than gain frames; positive frames increase acceptance of treatment — so a single frame can quietly steer decisions.
- **Example:** Asked to pick the "half-empty" glass people chose the previously-full one; asked for "half-full" they chose the previously-empty one — proving the frames encode the situation's dynamics.

### [RFM:18] Embrace uncertainty over the illusion of certainty  (ch 10)
- **Essence:** Statistical/skeptical thinking means abandoning the quest for certain knowledge and instead dealing rationally with an uncertain world via probabilities and expectancy (Pascal's wager, expected utility). The illusion of certainty is marketed (insurance, BSE-free beef, "you are infected for life") and demanded by patients/citizens, but modern tests and forensics are not certain.
- **Decision move:** Treat beliefs as bets to be assessed on evidence, not as certainties. When offered guaranteed safety or doom, ask for the underlying frequencies and error rates; expose where the "certainty" hides a base rate.
- **Applies / fails when:** Applies broadly — medicine, finance, politics, forensic evidence. Requires courage (Kant's *Sapere aude*): questioning certainties often means questioning authority.
- **Example:** A positive low-risk HIV test means only ~50% infection probability via Bayes, yet counselors and brochures declared it "absolutely sure," with life-altering consequences.

### [RFM:19] Statistical thinking, not statistical rituals  (ch 10, 11)
- **Essence:** Statistics is a toolbox (descriptive stats, exploratory analysis, Bayes, Neyman-Pearson, Wald's sequential, Fisher), and rationality is choosing the right tool for the problem — self-reflexive judgment. The "null ritual" (always test a nil null at p<.05) is a mindless, inconsistent hybrid that even Fisher, Neyman, and Pearson would each reject.
- **Decision move:** Match method to problem; never apply one procedure automatically. Choose the representation/tool that fits the question's structure and the available knowledge (e.g., Bayes only when base rates are known).
- **Applies / fails when:** Applies to any inference or analysis. The ritual persists because confusion sustains it and because editors/curricula enforce it — recognizing the toolbox dissolves the ritual.
- **Example:** ~80–90% of statistics teachers and students endorsed at least one false interpretation of "p=.01," inheriting illusions from textbooks that present one hammer as all of statistics.

### [RFM:20] State the hypothesis before seeing the data  (ch 11)
- **Essence:** A significant result is meaningless unless the alternative hypothesis was specified beforehand (Feynman's conjecture). Searching data for what is significant, then testing on the same data, is overfitting and after-the-fact story-telling, not evidence. With large samples in non-experimental settings, a guessed directional difference is "confirmed" ~50% of the time regardless of truth (Meehl's conjecture).
- **Decision move:** Commit to predictions before collecting/looking at data; validate models on fresh samples, not the discovery sample. Distrust impressive p-values attached to undefined hypotheses or huge samples — report and weigh effect size, not just significance.
- **Applies / fails when:** Applies to all hypothesis testing and model evaluation. The danger peaks with flexible models, many parameters, and big non-experimental datasets.
- **Example:** Feynman's psychologist found rats "alternated" at p<.05 — but the pattern was spotted in the data; a fresh experiment failed. Waller confirmed ~46% of randomly-directed MMPI gender hypotheses, matching Meehl.
