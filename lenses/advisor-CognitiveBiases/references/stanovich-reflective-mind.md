# Rationality and the Reflective Mind — Keith E. Stanovich  ·  prefix [SRM]
> Intelligence (IQ) and rationality are distinct: smart people think and act irrationally because the mind is a miser whose autonomous defaults go unchecked, and a separate, measurable set of dispositions and knowledge ("mindware") governs whether they get overridden. Use this lens when a decider is bright but the decision still feels off, when "I'm too smart to be biased" is in play, or when you must diagnose *why* a reasoning error happened (not enough knowledge, didn't notice, or couldn't hold the override).

## Principles

### [SRM:01] Rationality is normative and continuous  (ch 1)
- **Essence:** In cognitive science, rationality is not Aristotle's binary "uses reason / doesn't." It is the degree to which a thought or action departs from the optimum defined by a normative model. Everyone is rational *to a degree*.
- **Decision move:** Stop asking "is this irrational?" and ask "how far is this choice from the best available model, and what is the cost of that gap?"
- **Applies / fails when:** Applies whenever a defensible normative standard exists (probability, expected value, logic). Fails when no agreed normative model exists — then "irrational" is a contested label, not a fact.
- **Example:** A choice that violates expected-value maximization by a little is "less than perfectly rational," not a sign the person is thoughtless.

### [SRM:02] Two kinds of rationality: epistemic and instrumental  (ch 1)
- **Essence:** Epistemic rationality = your beliefs map reality accurately. Instrumental rationality = your actions maximize goal satisfaction. They are separable; you can fail one while passing the other.
- **Decision move:** Split any decision audit into two questions — "Are my beliefs about the situation true?" and "Given those beliefs, do my actions best serve my goals?"
- **Applies / fails when:** Always applies as a diagnostic frame. The failure mode is conflating them — treating an action error as if it were a knowledge error (or vice versa) and fixing the wrong thing.
- **Example:** Accurate forecast of a project's odds (epistemic) but still sinking money in due to sunk cost (instrumental failure).

### [SRM:03] The cognitive miser  (ch 1–2)
- **Essence:** The brain trades power for cost. It defaults to fast, cheap Type 1 (and shallow Type 2) processing because deep processing is slow, effortful, and experienced as aversive. Most of the time the cheap answer is "close enough."
- **Decision move:** Treat full analysis as a scarce resource to be deliberately *spent* on the few high-stakes, non-recurring decisions (career, marriage, major investments) where heuristics give no useful signal.
- **Applies / fails when:** Miserly defaults are fine for high-frequency, low-stakes, recurring choices. They are dangerous exactly when the decision is rare, unprecedented, or where the 2% catastrophic case dominates lifetime outcomes.
- **Example:** ~20–30 life decisions out of thousands determine life satisfaction; each is unique, so implicit/evolutionary heuristics have had no chance to calibrate — yet the miser still reaches for them.

### [SRM:04] The tripartite mind  (ch 2)
- **Essence:** Beyond the usual two systems, Stanovich splits Type 2 into two control levels. (1) Autonomous mind = Type 1 defaults (TASS). (2) Algorithmic mind = the computational horsepower for sustained decoupling, indexed by IQ/fluid intelligence. (3) Reflective mind = goals and thinking dispositions that decide *whether* to engage the algorithmic mind.
- **Decision move:** When a smart person errs, locate the failure at the right level: is the *machinery* (algorithmic) weak, or did the *disposition to deploy it* (reflective) never fire? They need different fixes.
- **Applies / fails when:** Useful for diagnosis and training design. The algorithmic/reflective line is fuzzier than the autonomous/Type-2 line — don't treat the three as separate brain organs, only as hierarchies of control.
- **Example:** Two people fail the same task: one lacks the working-memory capacity to hold the override (algorithmic); the other has the capacity but never bothered to engage it (reflective).

### [SRM:05] Override needs both a caller and a doer  (ch 2,4)
- **Essence:** Overriding a Type 1 response is two distinct functions. The reflective mind *initiates* the call to override (a disposition); the algorithmic mind *executes* the inhibition and simulation (a capacity). Classic dual-process theory ignored the caller.
- **Decision move:** To debias, ensure both: install a trigger ("does this deserve a second look?") AND the capacity/time to actually run the alternative. Adding capacity is useless if nothing calls for its use.
- **Applies / fails when:** Explains why training rules alone fails — the rule sits unused unless a reflective disposition routinely summons it. Fails as a model only if you assume override is a single monolithic act.
- **Example:** A checklist (reflective trigger) forces a pause; the analyst then has to actually do the disjunctive reasoning (algorithmic execution). Either step missing → heuristic answer.

### [SRM:06] Cognitive decoupling is the engine of rationality  (ch 3)
- **Essence:** The key function of the algorithmic mind is to sustain *decoupled secondary representations* — copies of reality you can manipulate offline without confusing them with reality. This underwrites hypothetical thinking and simulating "what if" worlds. It is also what fluid-intelligence/working-memory tests largely measure.
- **Decision move:** Force explicit simulation of alternative worlds before committing — externalize them (write the branches down) so the decoupling does not collapse under load.
- **Applies / fails when:** Essential for novel problems requiring imagined alternatives. Costly and capacity-limited; under time pressure or fatigue decoupling degrades and thought reverts to the focal model.
- **Example:** Genuinely weighing "what if this assumption is false?" requires holding a false-world model apart from your believed world long enough to reason inside it.

### [SRM:07] Serial associative cognition with a focal bias  (ch 4)
- **Essence:** A trap *inside* Type 2 thinking. You reason slowly and consciously but never leave the first, easiest-to-build model. The focal model represents one state of affairs, accepts what's presented as true, minimizes effort, ignores moderators, and stays close to prior belief. It feels like real thinking but never branches to alternatives.
- **Decision move:** Detect it by the symptom — thought that "drills sideways" by association rather than enumerating an exhaustive set of relevant alternatives at each node. Counter with forced disjunctive reasoning: "list all the options here, then narrow."
- **Applies / fails when:** This is the mechanism behind framing effects (passive acceptance of the given formulation) and myside bias. The boundary: genuine simulation also branches, but only on *relevant* alternatives, not associative tangents.
- **Example:** Planning a trip by free-associating (broker → bus → sandwich shop → focaccia → bakery) vs. systematically enumerating and pruning the real alternatives for the day.

### [SRM:08] The Master Rationality Motive (MRM)  (ch 5)
- **Essence:** A high-level desire "to act in accordance with reasons" — the motive that drives the search for *rational integration* across your preference hierarchy. What is rational is not a particular integration algorithm but the felt *need* to integrate. Individual differences in the urge to make preferences cohere trace to differences in MRM strength.
- **Decision move:** Surface second- and third-order preferences ("do I want to want this?"). A live struggle between first- and second-order desires is the signal that genuine self-definition is happening — lean into it rather than suppressing the discomfort.
- **Applies / fails when:** Drives long-horizon self-improvement and value change. Weak MRM → preferences stay incoherent and externally manipulable. Not an automatic process; needs motivational force to start.
- **Example:** A smoker who "prefers his preference not to smoke" over his preference to smoke ratifies the higher-order judgment, increasing pressure to actually change behavior.

### [SRM:09] Neurathian preference repair  (ch 5)
- **Essence:** There is no privileged "highest" level or fixed "true self" to anchor value revision. Like repairing a boat at sea by standing on some planks while replacing others, you revise some values using others as temporary footing — but you risk standing on a rotten plank (a value itself corrupted by bad mindware/memes).
- **Decision move:** When values conflict, don't search for one foundational value to settle it. Iteratively test each value against the others; periodically audit whether the plank you're standing on is itself sound.
- **Applies / fails when:** The right model for adult value change and identity work. The hazard: using a contaminated belief as the criterion to judge all others — guarantees a stable but harmful equilibrium.
- **Example:** Revising career goals by leaning on your value of honesty — but if "honesty" has been co-opted into a self-punishing belief, the whole repair compounds the damage.

### [SRM:10] The taxonomy of rational-thinking errors  (ch 6, West)
- **Essence:** Every reasoning error falls into one of five causes: (1) **Default to the autonomous mind** (vividness, affect/attribute substitution, impulsive association — no Type 2 engaged); (2) **Focal bias / serial associative cognition** (Type 2 ran but never left the easy model); (3) **Override failure** (decoupling engaged but lost to the Type 1 pull — willpower/self-control); (4) **Mindware gap** (the rule was never learned); (5) **Contaminated mindware** (learned beliefs that harm). Categories 1–3 are miserliness; 4–5 are knowledge.
- **Decision move:** When auditing a bad decision, classify the cause *before* prescribing a fix — the five causes need different remedies (a trigger, an alternatives prompt, more capacity/time, teaching the rule, or uninstalling a belief).
- **Applies / fails when:** Organizes the entire heuristics-and-biases literature. Boundaries are fuzzy (a half-learned rule sits between gap and override failure); use it as a diagnostic grid, not airtight bins.
- **Example:** A conjunction-fallacy error is "default to autonomous" for most people (representativeness fired, no struggle) but "override failure" for the rare expert who consciously fights the homunculus and still loses.

### [SRM:11] Mindware gap  (ch 6)
- **Essence:** A failure caused by *missing* knowledge — the rules and strategies of rational thought (probability, falsification, alternative-hypothesis testing, regression to the mean) were never learned or never compiled well enough to support override.
- **Decision move:** Before blaming bias or willpower, check whether the person actually possesses the relevant rule. If not, the fix is teaching/installing mindware, not motivation.
- **Applies / fails when:** Implicated wherever a domain rule is specialized (e.g., probabilistic reasoning in gambling). Counterintuitive corollary: the *fewer* gaps someone has, the more their remaining errors are attributable to miserliness rather than ignorance.
- **Example:** A gambler who has never learned about the gambler's fallacy or regression to the mean isn't being lazy — the corrective mindware simply isn't installed.

### [SRM:12] Contaminated mindware  (ch 6)
- **Essence:** Not all acquired knowledge helps. Some installed beliefs/strategies directly cause goal-thwarting irrationality. Especially dangerous are *evaluation-disabling* properties — beliefs that protect themselves via threats for questioning, rewards for faith, or unfalsifiability — plus an egocentric "self" model that fuels myside/focal bias.
- **Decision move:** Probe any high-conviction belief for self-protecting structure: Does it punish doubt? Promise reward for faith? Resist any disconfirming test? Those flags mark mindware worth quarantining, regardless of how it feels.
- **Applies / fails when:** Explains why intelligent people hold and defend harmful ideas — IQ helps *justify* contaminated mindware, not reject it. The line with "mindware gap" is fuzzy (e.g., probability misconceptions can be either).
- **Example:** A belief system that says "doubting this is itself proof of corruption" is contaminated mindware: it disables the very evaluation that would expose it.

### [SRM:13] Override detection precedes override  (ch 7)
- **Essence:** Distinguish errors of *comprehension* (you don't know the norm = mindware gap) from errors of *application* (you know the norm but fail to notice the situation calls for it). The latter is "override detection failure" — most everyday biases are this: people would endorse the violated norm in hindsight but never detected the cue to apply it.
- **Decision move:** Engineer detection. Since the bottleneck is *noticing*, build environmental cues, checklists, or red-flag triggers that fire on situations needing analysis — don't just teach more rules.
- **Applies / fails when:** Dominant explanation when people "should have known better." Fails to apply when the rule genuinely isn't possessed (that's a comprehension/gap problem instead).
- **Example:** People who know what framing is still fall for framed choices because nothing flagged "this is a frame — check the complement."

### [SRM:14] The three-stage model of bias performance  (ch 7)
- **Essence:** Whether someone gives the normative answer runs through three sequential gates: (1) **Is the relevant mindware available?** If no → heuristic response (path 1). (2) **Is the need to override detected?** If no → heuristic response (path 2). (3) **Does the task require sustained decoupling, and does the person have the capacity?** If not → heuristic response (path 3). A bias correlates with IQ only when individual differences in capacity gate the outcome at stage 3.
- **Decision move:** To predict or fix performance on a given task, find which gate is binding — installing mindware, prompting detection, or freeing up capacity each targets a different gate.
- **Applies / fails when:** Explains *why* some biases correlate with cognitive ability (capacity-gated) and others don't (gated at detection, which IQ doesn't fix). Assumes the task pits a heuristic against a normative response.
- **Example:** Belief bias correlates with IQ (needs sustained decoupling); many framing/default errors don't, because the binding gate is detection, where smarts give little edge.

### [SRM:15] Dysrationalia — intelligence weakly predicts rationality  (ch 7,9)
- **Essence:** Empirically, associations between IQ and performance on heuristics-and-biases tasks are surprisingly *modest*, and highly variable across tasks. Some major biases (myside bias, anchoring, certain framing/default effects) are nearly independent of cognitive ability. Smart people are not protected.
- **Decision move:** Never treat a decider's intelligence, credentials, or expertise as evidence their reasoning is unbiased. Run the same debiasing protocol regardless of IQ — and watch especially for the "I'm too smart to be biased" blind spot (a contaminated lay-psychology belief).
- **Applies / fails when:** Holds across the bias literature. IQ *does* help on capacity-gated tasks (stage 3 above), so "intelligence is irrelevant" overstates it — it's a weak, selective predictor, not a null one.
- **Example:** High-IQ respondents show roughly the same myside bias as low-IQ respondents; the bright reasoner just builds more elaborate justifications for the same one-sided model.

### [SRM:16] Don't deify intelligence; keep rationality named separately  (ch 9)
- **Essence:** Folding rationality, wisdom, and critical thinking into a broad "intelligence" makes the concept vacuous ("everything the brain does") and, worse, inflates IQ tests further while erasing the imperative to assess and train rationality. Stanovich argues for a *narrow* IQ concept (it indexes algorithmic decoupling — a real, important joint of nature) precisely so rationality keeps its own conceptual space.
- **Decision move:** In any evaluation of "good thinking," keep rationality as a separate, explicitly named target. Don't let "he's brilliant" stand in for "he reasons well" — they're different scorecards.
- **Applies / fails when:** A framing discipline for assessment, hiring, and education. The argument is conceptual/strategic, not a claim that IQ is unimportant — narrow IQ is highly valid for what it measures.
- **Example:** Calling all good cognitive traits "intelligence" is like calling braking and comfort "horsepower" — it makes the thing you wanted to de-emphasize even more dominant.

### [SRM:17] Rationality is assessable — toward an RQ  (ch 10)
- **Essence:** There's no in-principle barrier to a "rationality quotient." Rational thought decomposes into operationalizable components: **fluid rationality** (resistance to miserly processing — belief bias, attribute substitution, the Cognitive Reflection Test, disjunctive reasoning; plus resistance to myside thinking and to irrelevant context/framing) and **crystallized mindware** (facilitators like probabilistic reasoning, and the *absence* of contaminated mindware/inhibitors).
- **Decision move:** To strengthen a decider (or yourself), target named components with their known paradigms: train CRT-style "stop and check" reflexes, run disjunctive-reasoning drills, audit affective forecasting, test for myside bias with argument-evaluation tasks.
- **Applies / fails when:** Gives a concrete training/assessment map for rationality. Limitation: components are many and partly correlated; no single test captures all of it — rationality is "multifarious," so use a battery, not one number.
- **Example:** The bat-and-ball CRT item ("$1.10 total, bat costs a dollar more — how much is the ball?") cheaply detects whether someone overrides the seductive intuitive answer ($0.10) for the correct one ($0.05).
