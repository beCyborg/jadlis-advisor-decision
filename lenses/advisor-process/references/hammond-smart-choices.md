# Smart Choices: A Practical Guide to Making Better Decisions — Hammond, Keeney & Raiffa  ·  prefix [SC]
> A good decision is defined by its *process*, not its outcome; resolve any hard choice by decomposing it into eight elements (PrOACT + uncertainty, risk tolerance, linked decisions) and reassembling. Use this lens whenever a decision feels tangled, high-stakes, or stuck — when the decider can't see what to focus on or how to compare unlike alternatives.

## Principles

### [SC:01] Decide on process, not outcome  (ch 1, 7)
- **Essence:** A good decision can have a bad outcome and vice versa, because uncertainty intervenes. Quality lives in *how* you decide, not in how it turns out.
- **Decision move:** Judge and defend choices by the soundness of the process used, not by results. When reviewing, distinguish "smart choice, bad consequence" (was fine) from "poor choice, good consequence" (still a mistake).
- **Applies / fails when:** Essential under uncertainty; less salient for near-certain decisions where consequences are knowable in advance.
- **Example:** Lee starts construction in mild-winter NC on good forecasts; a freak 40-year winter adds $6k. Still a smart choice — he could not have known.

### [SC:02] Be proactive; cycle the eight elements  (ch 1, 11)
- **Essence:** Any complex decision decomposes into eight elements — Problem, Objectives, Alternatives, Consequences, Tradeoffs (PrOACT), plus Uncertainty, Risk tolerance, Linked decisions. Divide and conquer, then reassemble.
- **Decision move:** Run a quick "fire-drill" scan of all eight in minutes/hours; identify the one or two elements actually blocking you and concentrate effort there. Don't analyze every element in depth.
- **Applies / fails when:** Universal scaffold; the depth applied to each element scales with stakes. Over-processing trivial decisions wastes energy.
- **Example:** Bill's "sell my business" decision reverses to "don't sell, open a West Coast branch" once objectives, alternatives, and tradeoffs are examined.

### [SC:03] Frame the right problem  (ch 2)
- **Essence:** How you state the problem frames everything — the alternatives you see and how you evaluate them. A good solution to a poorly posed problem is worse than a decent solution to the right one.
- **Decision move:** Write the initial problem statement, then question, test, and rephrase it. Ask "Am I working on the right problem?" repeatedly, especially when info or circumstances change.
- **Applies / fails when:** Highest leverage early; revisit at transitions. The danger is laziness — accepting the first/obvious framing.
- **Example:** Bob, told to pick among out-of-state analyst jobs, frames it as "How do I stay with my employer?" instead of "best analyst job in Portland?" — and ends up with a worse job and a nightmare commute.

### [SC:04] Question triggers and constraints  (ch 2)
- **Essence:** Every decision has a trigger that can bias framing, and embedded constraints that silently narrow options. Many constraints are assumed, not real.
- **Decision move:** Name the trigger and the assumed constraints. Recast "When/which X?" into "Why X at all? / How else?" Try assuming a constraint is gone and see what alternatives appear.
- **Applies / fails when:** Powerful when a problem feels boxed-in; some constraints are genuinely binding and must stay (as objectives, not framing limits).
- **Example:** "When do we meet?" can't be scheduled → recast to "How do we finish the strategy?" → solved via email + conference calls, no meeting needed.

### [SC:05] Specify fundamental objectives  (ch 3)
- **Essence:** Objectives are what you really want. Means objectives are way-stations; fundamental objectives are wanted for their own sake. Repeatedly asking "Why?" peels means away to reach ends.
- **Decision move:** Brainstorm all concerns, phrase each as verb+object ("minimize cost"), then ask "Why?" until you hit bedrock. Then ask "What do I really mean by this?" to clarify each.
- **Applies / fails when:** Critical because objectives are the decision criteria; a means objective in one problem is fundamental in another. Skipping this produces unbalanced decisions (hard drives out soft).
- **Example:** EPA: "minimize emissions" → why? → reduce concentrations → limit exposure → protect health. Health is the fundamental end.

### [SC:06] Objectives are decision criteria  (ch 3)
- **Essence:** Only fundamental objectives should be used to evaluate alternatives; means objectives are for *generating* them. Don't let easy-to-measure stand in for what matters.
- **Decision move:** Use means objectives to spark alternatives (ask "How?"); use fundamental objectives to compare them. Don't limit objectives to what data is available.
- **Applies / fails when:** Always; the trap is "looking for the wallet under the streetlight" — choosing measurable-but-irrelevant criteria.
- **Example:** Use "daily commute time" not the easier-to-measure "distance from work" when deciding where to live.

### [SC:07] Generate creative alternatives  (ch 4)
- **Essence:** Alternatives are the raw material — you can never choose one you didn't consider, and your choice is only as good as the best of the set. Most decisions suffer from a narrow option set.
- **Decision move:** Use objectives (ask "How?"), challenge constraints, set high aspirations, think alone first, learn from experience, ask others, give the subconscious time. Create first, evaluate later; never stop looking.
- **Applies / fails when:** High payoff almost always; avoid "business as usual," incrementalizing, default options, first-solution, and other-supplied menus. Balance effort against quality — know when to quit.
- **Example:** A utility assumes its power plant must be on a waterway ($1.5B+, big environmental harm); dropping the assumed constraint reveals an inland site, $1.2B, minimal harm.

### [SC:08] Use special-purpose alternatives  (ch 4)
- **Essence:** Four alternative types fit particular problems: *process* (a fair mechanism vs. a choice), *win-win* (satisfy the other decider's objectives), *information-gathering* (reduce uncertainty), *time-buying* (defer with partial commitment).
- **Decision move:** When relationships matter, use a process (vote, coin toss, arbitration). When you need approval, engineer a win-win. When uncertain, list info-gathering options. When the deadline may be soft, find a halfway/lease-with-option move.
- **Applies / fails when:** Match type to problem structure; time-buying must offer real benefit, not just avoidance.
- **Example:** Two roommates both want the spare skating ticket → flip a coin (process alternative) preserves the friendship better than choosing.

### [SC:09] Describe consequences in a table  (ch 5)
- **Essence:** To compare alternatives you must describe, for each, how well it meets each objective — with appropriate *accuracy, completeness, and precision*. A consequences table (objectives down, alternatives across) imposes this discipline.
- **Decision move:** Mentally inhabit the future ("imagine you have chosen it"); write free-form descriptions; build the matrix using consistent scales per row (numbers or words). "Try before you buy." Use experts for what-is, but learn their reasoning.
- **Applies / fails when:** Always valuable for multi-objective choices; pick precision/scales that reflect real accuracy — neither false precision ($33,475) nor masking detail (A/B/C buckets).
- **Example:** Vincent charts five jobs against salary, benefits, vacation, enjoyment, flexibility, skills in one table — turning scattered notes into easy comparison.

### [SC:10] Eliminate dominated alternatives  (ch 5, 6)
- **Essence:** If A is better than B on some objectives and no worse on all others, B is *dominated* — drop it. "Practical dominance" allows dropping B when its sole tiny advantage is clearly outweighed.
- **Decision move:** Convert the consequences table into a ranking table (rank options 1..n per objective), scan columns, and eliminate dominated/practically-dominated options before doing hard tradeoffs.
- **Applies / fails when:** Pure tradeoff-saver; the fewer the options, the fewer the swaps. Sometimes leaves a single survivor = the answer.
- **Example:** Alan's office search: Lombard dominates current Pierpoint (better on 4, ties on 1) → Pierpoint eliminated immediately.

### [SC:11] Make tradeoffs by even swaps  (ch 6)
- **Essence:** Conflicting objectives force tradeoffs across unlike scales ("apples, oranges, and elephants"). An even swap increases an option's value on one objective while decreasing it by an equivalent amount on another — until an objective is equal across all options and can be canceled.
- **Decision move:** Determine the change needed to equalize one objective; find the compensating change on another; make the swap; cancel the now-irrelevant objective; re-check for newly-dominated options. Iterate (swap to kill objectives, dominance to kill options) until one remains.
- **Applies / fails when:** The core multi-objective method; mechanics are easy, value judgments are the hard part. Generalizes Ben Franklin's "prudential algebra" to any number of options.
- **Example:** Cola company: swap "not franchising's" $15M profit edge for a 3% share gain → both equal on profit → franchising dominates on share → choose franchising.

### [SC:12] Swap on amount, not perceived importance  (ch 6)
- **Essence:** It's meaningless to call one objective "more important" without considering the *range* of variation across the actual options. Value of an increment depends on what you start with.
- **Decision move:** When swapping, focus on the concrete amounts in play (this much money for that much time), not abstract priority labels. Make easier swaps first; keep swaps consistent (if A↔B and B↔C then A↔C); seek info to inform swaps.
- **Applies / fails when:** Resolves stalemated "X matters more!" debates by anchoring on quantities; requires honest self-assessment of value.
- **Example:** Library hours vs. budget: cutting 2 hrs/week saving $250k flips the advocate; saving only $25k flips the watchdog. The amount decides.

### [SC:13] Build risk profiles for uncertainty  (ch 7)
- **Essence:** When consequences can't be known until after choosing, capture each alternative's uncertainty as a *risk profile* answering four questions: key uncertainties, possible outcomes, chances, consequences.
- **Decision move:** List uncertainties → keep only those that materially change consequences → define mutually-exclusive, collectively-exhaustive, unambiguous outcomes → assign chances → describe consequences. Often the profile alone reveals the choice.
- **Applies / fails when:** For decisions where uncertainty looms large (investing, insurance, medical, legal). Skip full treatment for minor uncertainties — use a representative estimate.
- **Example:** Joe's bid profile (no/partial/full contract with chances + consequences) shows partial & full are both likely and far better than the status quo → he bids.

### [SC:14] Quantify chances; resist vague words  (ch 7)
- **Essence:** "Fairly likely" means different things to different people. Numeric probabilities reduce miscommunication and sharpen decisions; precision is rarely needed beyond a useful range.
- **Decision move:** Assign chances via judgment, existing data, new data, experts, or by decomposing the uncertainty into components. Pin down reluctant estimators by "zeroing in from the extremes" (more than 25%? more than 50%? as high as 90%?). Probabilities must sum to 100%.
- **Applies / fails when:** Quantify for decisions justified to others; qualitative may suffice for private calls. Test sensitivity — often the decision is stable across a chance range.
- **Example:** Lawyer Sam won't put a number on winning; pressed from the extremes he settles on "70% is a good estimate," which sharpens Karen's analysis.

### [SC:15] Picture choices with decision trees  (ch 7, 9)
- **Essence:** A decision tree graphically lays out alternatives (squares), uncertainties (circles), outcomes (branches w/ chances), and consequences (tips) — a blueprint of the decision's architecture.
- **Decision move:** Sketch the tree; for linked problems, get the *timing* right and solve by working backward from the right (decide each future node, prune un-taken branches) to the present basic decision.
- **Applies / fails when:** Indispensable for complex/uncertain or linked choices and for explaining decisions to others; keep trees simple, add detail only as needed.
- **Example:** Janet's party tree (hotel vs. picnic × rain/shine, 30% rain) makes it obvious a great picnic is worth a 30% rain risk.

### [SC:16] Account for risk tolerance separately  (ch 8)
- **Essence:** Risk tolerance is your willingness to accept downside in pursuit of upside — as personal as personality. Most people are risk-averse: poor consequences weigh more than equally-likely good ones.
- **Decision move:** (1) Think hard about the *relative desirability* of consequences; (2) *weight* desirabilities by their chances; (3) compare and choose. Keep this separate from probability estimates.
- **Applies / fails when:** Needed when risk profiles don't yield an obvious winner. The smart choice for one person differs from another's — never let an advisor's risk attitude override yours.
- **Example:** Rob's cataract surgery: failure slightly worse than success is good, but success is 9× more likely → he schedules surgery. Karen, unlike her lawyer, settles because losing the trial would ruin her.

### [SC:17] Score desirability to break ties  (ch 8)
- **Essence:** When qualitative thinking can't decide, quantify: assign desirability scores (0 worst, 100 best) to consequences, multiply each by its outcome's probability, sum to an overall score per alternative.
- **Decision move:** Rank consequences, score them, weight by chances, sum, choose the highest. For many numeric consequences use a *desirability curve* (utility curve) plotted from ~5 points; its upward bow = risk aversion, straight = neutral, downward = risk-seeking. Convert scores back to money for intuition (the gap from average monetary value = your "risk adjustment").
- **Applies / fails when:** Reserve for the most important/complex choices; overkill for routine ones. Curves require consequences expressible on a single numeric variable (use even swaps to collapse multiple objectives to one).
- **Example:** Marisa scores London 100 / NY 80 / Buenos Aires 50 / Santiago 0, weights by posting chances → accounting firm (72) beats consulting (62.5); she takes it.

### [SC:18] Reshape risk profiles to fit tolerance  (ch 8)
- **Essence:** When a good opportunity exceeds your comfort, you can transact with others to alter the risk profile rather than reject the opportunity.
- **Decision move:** Apply five levers — *share* the risk (bring in partners), *seek risk-reducing information*, *diversify* (don't concentrate), *hedge* (lock in prices/rates), *insure* against rare catastrophic downside (but don't over-insure what you can absorb).
- **Applies / fails when:** Especially financial/business risk; the question is "what transactions improve my risk profile?" Don't insure losses you can afford.
- **Example:** Gas driller Harry invests only $25k of a $125k well (shares), runs seismic tests (info), holds stocks/mutual funds (diversify), sells half his gas at fixed prices (hedge), insures against blowouts but not his pickup.

### [SC:19] Plan ahead on linked decisions  (ch 9)
- **Essence:** Linked decisions are ones where today's choice creates and shapes tomorrow's alternatives. The pattern is decide → learn → decide → learn. Like a chess player, plan a few moves ahead.
- **Decision move:** Six steps — (1) understand the basic decision + key uncertainties; (2) identify ways to reduce critical uncertainties (info decisions); (3) identify linked future decisions; (4) map relationships in a tree/timeline; (5) decide the basic decision by thinking ahead, working backward (info is only worth gathering if it could change the decision); (6) treat each later decision as a fresh problem when you reach it.
- **Applies / fails when:** For the hardest, most important decisions; look only to a natural horizon (~2 future decisions), keep it simple. Even modest foresight prevents the dumb choices.
- **Example:** Dan & Doris reduce a dozen uncertainties to a few, invent an "Explore" (6-month leave) info alternative, build a timeline + tree, quantify just two probabilities (DOD contract ~30%, DotCom permanence ~75%), and decide to take the new job outright.

### [SC:20] Keep options open with flexible plans  (ch 9)
- **Essence:** When uncertainty is too great to plan future moves with confidence, build flexibility into the plan itself.
- **Decision move:** Choose among four flexible forms — *all-weather* plans (good in most cases, optimal in none), *short-cycle* plans (decide, then reassess frequently), *option-wideners* (act to expand future alternatives), *"be-prepared"* backup plans (a reasonable response ready for most contingencies).
- **Applies / fails when:** Volatile, fast-developing situations (ER, firefighting, business). All-weather plans trade optimality for safety.
- **Example:** A computer maker buys 90% of chips from its main supplier and 5% each from two others — an option-widener that protects supply if the main line is cut.

### [SC:21] Disarm psychological traps by awareness  (ch 10)
- **Essence:** Mental heuristics misfire as predictable, invisible traps that corrupt even careful decisions. The best defense is awareness — "forewarned is forearmed."
- **Decision move:** Watch for and counter each: **Anchoring** (first info dominates → view from multiple starting points, think before consulting others); **Status-quo** (bias to do nothing → ask "would I pick this if it weren't current?"); **Sunk-cost** (justifying past spend → ignore unrecoverable costs, reassign the decision to someone uninvolved); **Confirming-evidence** (seeking support → play devil's advocate, weigh disconfirming data equally); **Framing** (gains vs. losses, reference points → reframe neutrally, combine gains+losses); **Overconfidence** (ranges too narrow → start from extremes, challenge experts); **Recallability** (vivid events overweighted → use statistics); **Base-rate** neglect (→ consider base rates explicitly); **Prudence** (slanting "to be safe" cascades → state honest estimates); **Outguessing randomness** & **surprised-by-surprises** (→ accept randomness; rare events happen to *someone*).
- **Applies / fails when:** Highest-stakes, most-assumption-laden decisions are most vulnerable, especially those involving uncertainty (we lack feedback to calibrate probability judgments).
- **Example:** Framing made a $200M difference: NJ defaulted drivers to "limited right to sue" (80% kept it); PA defaulted to "full right" (only 25% chose limited) — same choice, different frame.

### [SC:22] Use advisors for facts, decide values yourself  (ch 11)
- **Essence:** Experts know "what is / what might be"; you alone know your values, objectives, tradeoffs, and risk tolerance. Asking "What should I do?" surrenders the parts only you can supply.
- **Decision move:** Solicit advisors' input on problem definition, alternatives, consequences, and uncertainties — then combine with your own values and decide yourself. If you do want their recommendation, give them your objectives, tradeoffs, and risk tolerance first.
- **Applies / fails when:** Any decision beyond your expertise; the discomfort of following advice blindly signals the values weren't yours. Also: get started early (beat procrastination), concentrate on what's important, chip at complexity (layers, zoom in/out, consistent bundles, right level of detail), get unstuck (talk it out; advise a hypothetical other; remove the obstacle imaginatively), know when to quit (balance effort vs. marginal gain), set standing principles for routine choices, and periodically tune your decision style.
- **Example:** Keith, paralyzed over leaving home for Olympic training, is asked "what would you advise someone else in your exact situation?" — instantly: "Train with the best; leave home if you have to." And he does.
