# Decisive: How to Make Better Choices in Life and Work — Chip & Dan Heath  ·  prefix [DEC]
> Good decisions come from a repeatable *process* (WRAP), not from raw analysis or gut — install a four-step process that counteracts the four predictable villains of decision making. Use this lens when a deliberate choice (job, hire, strategy, relationship, budget) takes more than five minutes and the environment is too noisy or infrequent to trust intuition.

## Principles

### [DEC:01] Process over analysis (WRAP)  (ch 1)
- **Essence:** Understanding our biases doesn't fix them; only a process does. A study of 1,048 business decisions found that decision *process* mattered more than analysis "by a factor of six." The pros-and-cons list — Franklin's "moral algebra" — is the only process in wide use and it counteracts none of the villains.
- **Decision move:** Run any non-trivial choice through the WRAP loop: **W**iden your options, **R**eality-test your assumptions, **A**ttain distance before deciding, **P**repare to be wrong. Steps are sequential but not rigid; double back when you learn something.
- **Applies / fails when:** Applies to deliberate choices taking longer than five minutes. Fails for split-second intuitive calls in trained, high-feedback domains (chess master, NFL quarterback) — there, trust the gut.
- **Example:** Joseph Priestley's 1772 job decision: he ignored Franklin's pros/cons and instead widened options (negotiated terms), sought people who knew the lord, got distance from the salary excitement, and bought insurance against being wrong (£150/year for life).

### [DEC:02] The four villains & the spotlight  (ch 1)
- **Essence:** A "spotlight" effect (Kahneman's "what you see is all there is") makes us draw conclusions from only the readily available information. Each decision stage has a matching villain: encountering a choice → **narrow framing**; analyzing options → **confirmation bias**; making the choice → **short-term emotion**; living with it → **overconfidence**.
- **Decision move:** Name which villain is most active right now, then deploy the matching WRAP step. Switch from "auto spotlight" to manual: deliberately sweep the light into hidden corners.
- **Applies / fails when:** Universal diagnostic; the villains operate even on sophisticated experts. Hardest to spot in yourself — easier as an outside adviser.
- **Example:** "Should Shannon fire Clive or not?" feels answerable instantly, but the spotlight hides reframes (change his role, pair him with a mentor) and questions (is Shannon a bad manager?).

### [DEC:03] Escape the narrow frame  (ch 2)
- **Essence:** We define choices too narrowly and binary ("Should I break up or not?"). Nutt's research: only ~29% of organizational decisions considered more than one alternative — about the same as teenagers (30%). Whether-or-not framing should set off warning bells.
- **Decision move:** Reframe any "whether or not / this or that" question into "what are the ways I could…?" As an adviser, treat binary framing as the loudest red flag.
- **Applies / fails when:** Applies whenever a choice is stated as a single option to accept/reject. Watch that widening doesn't stall into analysis paralysis (see DEC:05 — one or two extra options is enough).
- **Example:** Quaker lost $1.5B on the Snapple acquisition partly through narrow framing; a teenager asks "go to the party or not?" instead of "how do I have fun this weekend?"

### [DEC:04] Opportunity cost & the vanishing-options test  (ch 2)
- **Essence:** Two cheap tools pry open a narrow frame. Opportunity cost: ask what else the same money/time/attention could buy (Eisenhower: one bomber = a modern school in 30+ cities). Vanishing-options test: imagine your current options are forbidden — what would you do then?
- **Decision move:** Before committing a resource, list what else it could buy. If stuck between A and B, ask "if I couldn't choose either, what else could I do?" — this forces the spotlight to move.
- **Applies / fails when:** Strong for purchases, resource allocation, and feeling trapped between two choices. Less needed when the option set is already genuinely rich.
- **Example:** Margaret Sanders, deciding whether to fire her introverted receptionist Anna, ran the vanishing-options test and discovered a better option than firing her.

### [DEC:05] Multitrack options  (ch 3)
- **Essence:** Consider more than one option simultaneously. Designers who developed ads in parallel scored higher on creativity *and* effectiveness; a German firm that weighed two or more alternatives made six times as many "very good" decisions. Multitracking reveals the "shape" of the problem, keeps egos unattached to a single option, gives a built-in fallback, and can be faster.
- **Decision move:** Always carry at least two live options into evaluation — but only one or two extra, not 24 (avoid choice overload). Develop them in parallel, not sequentially.
- **Applies / fails when:** Powerful for design, hiring, vendor choice, strategy. Guard against decision paralysis from too many options.
- **Example:** Lexicon (named BlackBerry, Pentium, Swiffer) assigns each naming brief to multiple small teams, including an "excursion team" attacking a distant analogous task.

### [DEC:06] Prevention vs promotion mindsets  (ch 3)
- **Essence:** A prevention focus aims to avoid negative outcomes; a promotion focus aims to pursue positive ones. We default to one. Companies that used *both* mindsets performed much better through and after a recession.
- **Decision move:** Deliberately generate options from each mindset: "What must I protect against?" *and* "What could I gain / make better?" Build the toggle into recurring decisions (e.g., budget cuts: "cut more here to invest more there").
- **Applies / fails when:** Applies to strategy, budgeting, personal goals — anywhere you've framed the problem purely defensively or purely aspirationally.
- **Example:** Doreen was only trying to limit work stress until her husband Frank prompted her to also ask how she could boost her happiness.

### [DEC:07] Avoid sham options  (ch 3)
- **Essence:** Beware fake alternatives staged to make one option look obvious (Kissinger's "nuclear war, present policy, or surrender"). Real options provoke real disagreement on the team.
- **Decision move:** Diagnostic — if everyone on the team agrees on which option wins, you probably have sham options; engineer genuinely competitive alternatives until there's honest disagreement.
- **Applies / fails when:** Critical in group/political settings where a decision is being rationalized rather than made. Less relevant for solo, low-stakes choices.
- **Example:** A staffer who presents the boss two terrible alternatives plus their preferred one is offering sham options, not real choice.

### [DEC:08] Find someone who solved your problem  (ch 4)
- **Essence:** When you need options but feel stuck, don't invent — sample the world's buffet. Three places to look: **outside** (competitive analysis, benchmarking, best practices), **inside** (your own "bright spots" — where things already work), and **in the distance** (laddering up via analogies; lower rungs = close/low-risk, higher rungs = distant/novel).
- **Decision move:** Ask "Who has already faced this?" Scope competitors, scale your internal bright spots, and ladder up the abstraction ("what else moves fast / retains customers / etc.").
- **Applies / fails when:** Applies whenever the problem is not truly novel (most aren't). Less useful for genuinely first-of-kind problems with no analogues.
- **Example:** Sam Walton copied a checkout solution by scoping a rival store; Kaiser scaled a sepsis protocol from one of its own hospitals; Fiona Fairhurst designed a faster swimsuit by studying sharks and torpedoes.

### [DEC:09] Decision playlists  (ch 4)
- **Essence:** A checklist stops people from making an error; a *playlist* stimulates new ideas. Encode your best recurring prompts so you proactively generate options instead of reacting.
- **Decision move:** For decisions you face repeatedly, build a short prompt-list ("Have we considered X? Could we ladder up? Prevention or promotion?") and run it every time.
- **Applies / fails when:** Best for recurring decision types (hiring, creative briefs, budget cuts). Not a substitute for judgment on novel one-offs.
- **Example:** Advertisers Hughes and Johnson use a playlist to spark many creative directions quickly rather than waiting for inspiration.

### [DEC:10] Consider the opposite  (ch 5)
- **Essence:** Confirmation bias makes us hunt for self-serving confirming information — "the single biggest problem in business" (Lovallo) because it looks scientific. The cure is manufactured disagreement: devil's advocate, murder boards, "consider the opposite."
- **Decision move:** Ask Roger Martin's question: "What would have to be true for this option to be the very best choice?" Spark constructive dissent before committing. For a personal version, "assume positive intent" to consider the opposite of an instinctive negative read.
- **Applies / fails when:** Essential when you already have a favored option and are "collecting data." Watch that licensed dissent stays constructive, not performative.
- **Example:** A frustrated spouse keeping a marriage diary discovers their partner isn't always selfish — the data contradicts the assumption.

### [DEC:11] Ask disconfirming questions  (ch 5)
- **Essence:** To get trustworthy information, ask questions designed to surface problems, not reassurance. We pretend to want truth ("Do these jeans make me look fat?") while seeking comfort.
- **Decision move:** Pose pointed disconfirming questions ("Who were the last three people to leave? What problems does this product have?"). But in power-imbalanced settings, switch to open-ended questions — probing ones can intimidate the weaker party into telling you what you want.
- **Applies / fails when:** Use direct disconfirming questions among peers/vendors. Use open-ended questions (doctor–patient, boss–subordinate) where power asymmetry would distort answers.
- **Example:** Prospective associates should ask a law firm "Who were the last three associates to leave, and how can I contact them?" — not "Is this a good place to work?"

### [DEC:12] Zoom out, zoom in  (ch 6)
- **Essence:** The **outside view** (base rates — how things generally unfold for situations like yours) is more accurate, but we gravitate to the **inside view** (our sense of our special situation). A **close-up** then adds texture the averages miss.
- **Decision move:** Zoom out — find base rates, or ask an expert for them (experts estimate base rates well but predict poorly). Zoom in — get one vivid, concrete sample of reality (talk to actual customers/patients, "go to the genba").
- **Applies / fails when:** Applies to forecasts and high-stakes life choices (new job, college major, opening a restaurant). Fails if you let the inside view override clear base-rate evidence.
- **Example:** "Jack knows his Thai restaurant will be a hit" ignores grim restaurant base rates; FDR read a statistical "mail brief" *and* a sample of actual letters.

### [DEC:13] Ooch before you leap  (ch 7)
- **Essence:** "Ooch" = run small, cheap experiments to test a hypothesis instead of predicting the future (which we do badly — Tetlock: expert predictions beaten by simple base-rate extrapolation). Why predict when you can *know*?
- **Decision move:** Before a big commitment, design a small real-world test (work in the pharmacy before pharmacy school; pilot one customer; sell one car online; offer a short consulting contract instead of relying on an interview).
- **Applies / fails when:** Ideal for testable bets (markets, careers, hires). Counterproductive for situations needing full commitment — the person who already *knows* they need the degree but dreads it should commit, not ooch.
- **Example:** John Hanks at National Instruments validated wireless sensors with a "brick in a bucket" prototype in the Costa Rican jungle before approving a $2–3M program.

### [DEC:14] Overcome short-term emotion — 10/10/10  (ch 8)
- **Essence:** Fleeting emotions tempt long-term-bad choices. Two subtle emotions drive a status-quo bias: **mere exposure** (we like the familiar) and **loss aversion** (losses hurt ~2x more than equivalent gains please — the endowment effect: a mug owner won't sell for under $7.12 though wouldn't have paid over $2.87).
- **Decision move:** Apply **10/10/10**: how will I feel about this in 10 minutes, 10 months, 10 years? This forces future emotions to count as much as present ones and exposes status-quo bias.
- **Applies / fails when:** Apply to choices clouded by visceral pull (sales pressure, infatuation, fear of change). The aim is not to eliminate emotion but to discount the fleeting kind.
- **Example:** 10/10/10 tipped Annie toward saying "I love you" first to Karl; PayPal's decision to ditch its PalmPilot product was a no-brainer that nonetheless *felt* hard due to status-quo bias.

### [DEC:15] Advise-a-friend / observer's view  (ch 8)
- **Essence:** Distance reveals the most important factor; up close we drown in complexity and cycle endlessly. We see others' decisions more clearly than our own.
- **Decision move:** Ask "What would I tell my best friend to do here?" (the single most powerful question for personal decisions) or take an observer's stance ("What would our successors do?").
- **Applies / fails when:** Best for agonizing, emotionally entangled personal or political decisions. Less needed for clear-cut, low-emotion calls.
- **Example:** Andy Grove and Gordon Moore broke years of paralysis on exiting memory chips by asking what a new CEO brought in from outside would do — then doing it themselves.

### [DEC:16] Honor your core priorities  (ch 9)
- **Essence:** When distance still leaves a decision agonizing, it usually signals a conflict among core priorities — your long-term values, goals, aspirations. The goal isn't to kill emotion but to honor the emotions that count.
- **Decision move:** Identify and enshrine core priorities in advance so recurring dilemmas resolve consistently. Then go on the *offense* against lesser priorities: keep a "stop-doing list" (Jim Collins) and protect time for what matters most.
- **Applies / fails when:** Applies to repeated value-laden trade-offs (org strategy, work-life balance). Knowing priorities ≠ living them — MIT managers had done zero work on stated priorities the prior week.
- **Example:** Interplast settled recurring debates once it declared the *patient* the ultimate customer; the USS Benfold crew fought low-value "List B" chores (used rust-free bolts to avoid repainting) to free time for real priorities.

### [DEC:17] Bookend the future  (ch 10)
- **Essence:** The future is a range, not a point. Estimate a lower bookend (dire scenario) and upper bookend (rosy scenario); predictions get more accurate when you stretch the bookends outward.
- **Decision move:** Run a **premortem** for the low end ("It's a year out, this failed utterly — why?"), a **preparade** for the high end ("We're heroes — are we ready to handle success?"), and add a **safety factor / buffer** for the truly unforeseeable. Anticipating problems also helps you cope when they hit.
- **Applies / fails when:** Applies to launches, investments, plans with real uncertainty. The bookends are "very bad / very good with real assumptions," not the most extreme outcomes imaginable.
- **Example:** Investor Penstock bet on Coinstar when bookending showed far more upside than downside; Softsoap's maker, anticipating a huge launch, locked up the plastic-pump supply for 18–24 months; the "realistic job preview" vaccinates new hires against later dissatisfaction.

### [DEC:18] Set a tripwire  (ch 11)
- **Essence:** We drift onto autopilot, leaving past decisions unquestioned, especially when change is gradual. A tripwire — a pre-committed trigger (date, metric, budget partition, or pattern) — snaps us awake to realize a choice exists. Tripwires also create a safe space for risk-taking: they cap downside and quiet the mind until the trigger fires.
- **Decision move:** Pre-specify the conditions that will force a re-decision ("If X happens / by date Y / past $Z, we reconsider"). Use partitions to curb escalation of commitment to failing bets; watch for *unexpected success* as a trigger too.
- **Applies / fails when:** Especially valuable for slow-moving threats and sunk-cost traps. Partitioning suits self-control problems better than office workflows (a per-page printer prompt would spark mutiny).
- **Example:** David Lee Roth's brown-M&M clause (Article 126) flagged whether stagehands had read the safety contract; Zappos pays new hires $1,000 (later $4,000) to quit, creating a conscious fork — only 2% take it; Kodak could have set tripwires on digital-image adoption.

### [DEC:19] Trust the process / procedural justice  (ch 12)
- **Essence:** Group decisions carry an extra burden — they must be *seen* as fair. "Bargaining" (horse-trading until all sides can live with the choice) reliably improved decision success ("dramatic" effect, Nutt) and accelerates implementation despite costing time up front. Procedural justice matters so much that losers who perceive a fair process are nearly as content as winners who don't.
- **Decision move:** Build and visibly run a fair process: bargain, state the other side's position better than they could, and point out your own decision's flaws. A trusted process licenses bolder choices — and the elderly regret what they *didn't* do more than what they did.
- **Applies / fails when:** Essential for multi-stakeholder decisions where buy-in determines execution. Less relevant for purely solo choices (though the confidence benefit still applies).
- **Example:** Mediator Mnookin disarms opponents by restating their position better than they can; ARTS founder Matt D'Arrigo used a trustworthy process to reconcile serving local kids with national-impact aspirations.
