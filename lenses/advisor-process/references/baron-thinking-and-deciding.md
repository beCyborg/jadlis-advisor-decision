# Thinking and Deciding — Jonathan Baron  ·  prefix [TD]
> Good thinking is whatever best achieves the thinker's own goals; most errors come from biased favoritism toward possibilities we already hold, and the cure is actively open-minded thinking plus the formal discipline of probability and utility. Let this lens dominate when a decision is high-stakes, contestable, belief-laden, or where you suspect you are deciding to confirm what you already wanted.

## Principles

### [TD:01] Search-inference framework  (ch 1)
- **Essence:** All thinking — decisions, beliefs, goals — consists of searching for three objects (possibilities, evidence, goals) and inferring: using evidence, weighted by goals, to revise the strength of each possibility.
- **Decision move:** Make the three searches explicit. List the options, the criteria (goals), and the evidence; ask which of the three is under-supplied right now.
- **Applies / fails when:** Universal scaffold for any deliberation; least useful for reflexive or perceptual judgments that bypass deliberation.
- **Example:** Choosing an elective: friend suggests a course (possibility), "heavy workload" (evidence), "manageable load" (goal) → reject and resume search.

### [TD:02] Normative / descriptive / prescriptive  (ch 2)
- **Essence:** Three distinct models: normative = the ideal standard for achieving goals (e.g. probability theory), descriptive = how people actually think (often heuristics that misfire), prescriptive = teachable rules that close the gap.
- **Decision move:** When stuck, ask which level you are arguing about. "Is this what's optimal, what people do, or what advice helps?" Don't critique behavior without a separate standard.
- **Applies / fails when:** Clarifies any debate about reasoning; fails if you collapse "most people do X" into "X is correct" (the model isn't validated by behavior).
- **Example:** All birth-order sequences are equally likely (normative); people judge by typicality (descriptive); "equally-likely sequences are equally likely" (prescriptive rule).

### [TD:03] Rationality is method, not outcome  (ch 3)
- **Essence:** Rational thinking is whatever method best helps achieve the thinker's goals — it is a matter of degree, not the same as being correct, and need not be cold, self-interested, or excessive.
- **Decision move:** Evaluate the *process* you used (sufficient search, fair use of evidence), not whether the conclusion turned out true. Give weight to uneasy feelings as evidence.
- **Applies / fails when:** Applies even to goal-setting where no "correct" answer exists; the standard is always the decider's own goals, so it can't be imposed externally.
- **Example:** A surgeon who operated on good information made a rational decision even if the patient was the 1-in-1000 who died.

### [TD:04] Decision quality ≠ outcome quality  (ch 3)
- **Essence:** A good decision uses available information well; a good outcome is merely one you like. Luck links them loosely. Judging deciders by outcomes (outcome bias) holds people responsible for chance.
- **Decision move:** When reviewing a past choice, reconstruct what was knowable *then*. If the decision was sound but the result bad, extract no "lesson" — reaffirm the process.
- **Applies / fails when:** Critical for after-action reviews and judging advisers; fails as an excuse when the outcome reveals foreseeable information that was ignored.
- **Example:** Two advisers give identical advice on identical info; A's investment wins, B's loses — people rate A the better decision-maker, which is the bias.

### [TD:05] Actively open-minded thinking (AOT)  (ch 9)
- **Essence:** The book's signature prescription. Poor thinking favors the possibility we already hold (myside bias): too little search, overconfidence, ignoring counter-evidence. AOT actively seeks new possibilities, goals, and evidence *against* the favored view — in proportion to the question's importance, practiced in moderation.
- **Decision move:** After forming a tentative view, deliberately argue the other side and hunt for the strongest disconfirming evidence before concluding. Ask "why might I be wrong?"
- **Applies / fails when:** Most valuable on important, belief-laden, or moral questions and after a tentative commitment; over-applied it causes paralysis, and it's wasted where one side is genuinely settled.
- **Example:** The A+ student kept generating ocean-mineral allocation schemes, finding an objection to each, then revising — versus the one-sided student who defended a single plan.

### [TD:06] Order & neutral-evidence principles  (ch 9)
- **Essence:** Two normative checks on belief revision. Order principle: when the order of two pieces of evidence is uninformative, order shouldn't change final belief (violated by primacy effects). Neutral-evidence principle: evidence equally consistent with a belief and its converse shouldn't strengthen it (violated by biased assimilation and polarization).
- **Decision move:** If mixed or ambiguous evidence is making you *more* confident, stop — that is a red flag for biased assimilation. Discount evidence you "filtered" through prior belief.
- **Applies / fails when:** Useful diagnostic for self-monitoring and group debate; belief persistence isn't always irrational when counter-evidence is genuinely weak.
- **Example:** Capital-punishment partisans read the same mixed studies and both grew *more* extreme, each finding flaws only in the opposing study.

### [TD:07] Strong inference, not confirmation  (ch 7)
- **Essence:** Hypothesis testing should seek evidence that could falsify, and should anticipate alternative explanations *before* testing. Best practice (Platt): split candidate hypotheses roughly in half and rule one half out, rather than "boldly" testing a single favorite.
- **Decision move:** Before running a test or experiment, ask "what alternative explanation would survive a positive result?" Choose the test whose key assumptions are most likely true and that eliminates the most rivals.
- **Applies / fails when:** Core to diagnosis, science, debugging, due diligence; fails when hypotheses can't be cleanly falsified or one test confounds multiple causes.
- **Example:** Semmelweis ruled out crowding, the priest, delivery position one by one, then confirmed "cadaverous matter" via handwashing — but a confounded design would have misled him.

### [TD:08] Maximize expected utility  (ch 10)
- **Essence:** The fundamental normative model: decompose a decision into options, mutually-exclusive-and-exhaustive states, and outcomes; multiply each outcome's utility by its state's probability and sum. Choose the highest. Only utility *differences* and the probability of states matter (zero point and units are arbitrary).
- **Decision move:** Build the decision table. Crucially, some errors cost far more than others — set your action threshold from the *ratio* of error utilities, not from probability alone.
- **Applies / fails when:** The justification for decision analysis; a normative ideal, not a prescriptive recipe — calculating every decision is self-defeating, so use it for important ones and rely on good heuristics otherwise.
- **Example:** Pascal's wager and "guilty beyond reasonable doubt": if a false conviction is 10× worse than a false acquittal, convict only above ~.91 probability of guilt.

### [TD:09] Sure-thing principle & invariance  (ch 10–11)
- **Essence:** Sure-thing: if a state yields the same outcome regardless of your choice, that outcome is irrelevant to the choice. Invariance: equivalent descriptions of the same situation should produce the same choice. Violations of either are irrational (Allais paradox, framing).
- **Decision move:** Strip out outcomes common to all options before comparing. Re-describe the decision at least two ways; if your choice flips, you're being framed.
- **Applies / fails when:** Powerful for cleaning up gambles, negotiations, and policy comparisons; the principle assumes goals are fixed across states (if your goals themselves shift with the irrelevant state, it can legitimately bend).
- **Example:** People pick options 1 and 4 in the Allais paradox, which is impossible under any consistent utility assignment, because they ignore the shared column.

### [TD:10] Utility ≠ money; diminishing marginal utility  (ch 10)
- **Essence:** Utility is the extent of goal achievement — not pleasure, money, or happiness. The marginal utility of wealth declines (an extra dollar matters less as you have more), which produces rational risk aversion (a concave utility curve).
- **Decision move:** Convert money outcomes to goal-achievement before deciding. For amounts small relative to lifetime wealth, decide by expected *value* (risk aversion barely matters and averages out); reserve risk aversion for large, one-shot stakes.
- **Applies / fails when:** Explains insurance, fair-bet avoidance, and progressive taxation; declining marginal utility is real but is *not* the whole story of risk aversion (see TD:11–12, TD:14).
- **Example:** Nobody pays the infinite expected value of the St. Petersburg game; ~$3–4 is the most people will bet, because high winnings have low marginal utility.

### [TD:11] Reference points & loss aversion  (ch 11)
- **Essence:** Prospect theory (descriptive): people evaluate outcomes as changes from a reference point, not final states. Losses loom larger than equal gains (loss aversion), the value curve is concave for gains and convex for losses — so people are risk-averse for gains and risk-seeking for losses (reflection effect). The reference point is easily manipulated.
- **Decision move:** Detect the implicit reference point and test it. Hold one fixed reference state across decisions; reframe "loss" descriptions as gains and vice versa to neutralize the framing.
- **Applies / fails when:** Explains most framing effects; the reference-point part of prospect theory is robust and essentially unchallenged. Using it knowingly (caring about gains/losses) isn't irrational — only being *manipulated* by description is.
- **Example:** "200 saved" → most pick the safe program; the identical "400 die" → most pick the risky one (Asian-disease problem).

### [TD:12] Probability weighting — certainty & possibility effects  (ch 11)
- **Essence:** We distort stated probabilities (the π function): we overweight certainty (the certainty effect) and overweight very low probabilities (the possibility effect), while underweighting mid-range probabilities; below a threshold we treat risk as zero. Weighting is most sensitive near 0 and 1.
- **Decision move:** Beware decisions hinging on a move *to* certainty or *from* impossibility — these are where you most distort. Recognize that a described "certain" outcome is usually itself a gamble on further consequences.
- **Applies / fails when:** Explains buying both lotteries and insurance, and the Allais paradox; means the standard gamble method *mismeasures* utility (the π function corrupts it).
- **Example:** People reject seat belts at a .00000025 per-trip death risk (treated as zero) but accept them at the equivalent .01 lifetime risk.

### [TD:13] Ambiguity & aversion to missing information  (ch 11)
- **Essence:** People avoid options whose probabilities feel "unknown" (Ellsberg) and will even pay to avoid them — a sure-thing violation. Ambiguity is really a perception that important information is missing; it's a framing effect (any situation can be made ambiguous or not by how you describe it).
- **Decision move:** When ambiguity repels you, ask: is there missing information I could actually obtain, and is it worth the cost? If yes, gather it (rational); if unavailable, set the aversion aside and act on best evidence using the principle of insufficient reason.
- **Applies / fails when:** Good heuristic that points toward value-of-information; harmful when the information is genuinely unavailable and you still freeze or overpay for "known" risks.
- **Example:** Subjects prefer the urn with a known 1/3 chance over an equivalent "unknown" urn, and pay to avoid the ambiguous one — yet symmetric arguments show no real reason to prefer either.

### [TD:14] Anticipated regret & disappointment  (ch 11)
- **Essence:** We react emotionally to comparisons: regret/rejoicing (vs. the outcome of a different *choice*) and disappointment/elation (vs. a different *state* under the same choice). These feelings are real utilities and should be counted — but they arise from our attitudes toward risk and are partly controllable.
- **Decision move:** Ask whether you'll truly feel the regret *in addition* to the loss, and whether you can adopt a "philosophical" attitude (I made the best call on what I knew). If controllable, set the feeling aside and choose on the underlying merits; expand the analysis to include the option of controlling the emotion.
- **Applies / fails when:** Legitimate to include anticipated emotion (it reflects goals); regret has a self-regulatory function when you can learn and control outcomes. Beware "maximizer" regret that paralyzes choice among many options.
- **Example:** People who expect to learn a gamble's outcome avoid the risky option to dodge the regret of "I'd have won had I chosen otherwise" — regret outweighs rejoicing.

### [TD:15] MAUT & value-focused thinking  (ch 14)
- **Essence:** Multiattribute utility theory decomposes an option's value into separate attributes (one per goal), scores each, weights by goal importance, and sums. The first and most important step is *discovering the values* — and distinguishing fundamental values from means values, which connect to fundamentals only through (often false) beliefs.
- **Decision move:** Before scoring options, run a value search: wish-list, pros/cons of alternatives, consequences, others' perspectives; then ask "why do I want this?" repeatedly until you hit the fundamental goal — which is often easier to achieve directly. Correct false beliefs linking means to ends.
- **Applies / fails when:** Best for complex, multi-goal, multi-stakeholder decisions and policy; weak where attributes are deeply interdependent or fundamental values are genuinely unknowable.
- **Example:** Reframing "accident prevention" (means) as "prevent death and injury" (fundamental) shifted policy from traffic-law enforcement to seat belts and safe car design.

### [TD:16] Utilitarian moral analysis  (ch 16)
- **Essence:** Decisions affecting others extend utility theory: the best act maximizes total utility over all relevant people, counted impartially. Moral judgments are *impersonal* (apply to anyone in the same situation), so relativism ("right for me") isn't a real moral claim. Beware the naturalistic fallacy — deriving "ought" from "is" alone.
- **Decision move:** For any choice affecting others, sum consequences across all affected people as if their goals were yours. Test moral claims for hidden "is→ought" premises (natural, traditional, evolved) and make the premise explicit.
- **Applies / fails when:** Provides a single consistent yardstick for moral and prudential decisions; many people's intuitions (fairness, rights — ch 17) diverge from it, and those intuitions often grasp a real partial truth, so check them against the utilitarian whole.
- **Example:** "Artificial insemination is unnatural, therefore wrong" hides the unstated premise "only the natural is permissible" — which the same person violates by using telephones and cars.

### [TD:17] Sunk cost, status-quo, omission & commitment biases  (ch 12)
- **Essence:** A cluster of decision biases: sunk-cost (honoring past, unrecoverable investment), status-quo bias (sticking with the default via loss aversion), omission bias (fearing harms from action more than equal harms from inaction), and commitment effect (defending a choice because *you* made it, to feel like a good decider).
- **Decision move:** Ask "what would I choose if there were no status quo and nothing already invested?" Treat sunk costs as gone; weigh action and inaction harms symmetrically; notice when you're defending a choice to protect your self-image.
- **Applies / fails when:** Pervasive in escalating commitments, investments, careers, and policy; legitimate reasons to persist exist (TD:18) — these biases are the *illegitimate* extras on top.
- **Example:** Staw: students who personally made an initial $10M investment that went badly poured more ($13M) into the failing division than if a stranger had decided — defending their own past choice.

### [TD:18] Self-control across time  (ch 19)
- **Essence:** Present/future conflicts mirror social dilemmas — the future self is "distant" and underweighted, so we over-favor the present. Self-control means binding ourselves (Ulysses) by deciding before temptation. Each choice sets a *precedent* for similar future cases, so apparently trivial decisions create personal goals and policies.
- **Decision move:** Make policies, not just one-off plans; commit in advance where temptation is foreseeable. When choosing goals, weigh how much you'll value *having and achieving* the future goal, not just your present taste. Distinguish good reasons to stick to a plan (dependability, momentum, self-trust) from biased ones (TD:17).
- **Applies / fails when:** Governs habits, careers, long-term commitments, and goal-setting; over-binding is costly when circumstances genuinely warrant changing a failing plan.
- **Example:** Lending notes to one friend sets a precedent binding you toward a different friend's later request — small decisions silently become policies.

### [TD:19] Quantitative thinking about risk  (ch 20)
- **Essence:** Public and personal risk intuitions systematically oppose risks that are involuntary, catastrophic, unknown, caused by action, or "artificial," and crave reducing risks to zero — biases visible in both lab and real regulation. The antidote is quantitative thinking: good decisions require comparing *magnitudes* and tradeoffs.
- **Decision move:** Don't demand zero risk or privilege "natural" risks; estimate and compare the quantities (you needn't have exact numbers — a feel for relative magnitudes suffices to guess the right call). Trade off probability against payoff explicitly.
- **Applies / fails when:** Essential for safety, health, and policy decisions; on involuntary risks intuition and normative theory actually agree, so the bias correction matters most for voluntary/action/naturalness framings.
- **Example:** A tennis player intuitively trades off the probability of a shot going out against the probability of winning the point — the same quantitative logic should govern risk regulation.
