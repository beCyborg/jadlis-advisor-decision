# Sources of Power — Gary A. Klein  ·  prefix [SOP]
> Experts decide by recognizing situations and mentally simulating one option at a time, not by comparing many — so when you face a high-stakes, time-pressured, ill-defined call with real experience behind you, trust pattern + simulation over formal option-scoring.

This lens dominates when: the decider has genuine domain experience, time is short, goals are ill-defined, conditions shift, and stakes are high (naturalistic decision making). It recedes when the decider is a novice, the problem is well-defined and static, justification or optimization is required, or stakeholders must be reconciled — there, analysis earns its keep.

## Principles

### [SOP:01] Recognition-Primed Decision (RPD)  (ch 3)
- **Essence:** Experienced deciders size up a situation as a familiar prototype and immediately know a workable course of action — recognizing goals, cues, expectancies, and a typical response in one act. 80%+ of expert fireground decisions worked this way across 600+ coded decision points.
- **Decision move:** Ask "what kind of situation is this, and what does it typically call for?" Act on the first option that is acceptable rather than building an option set.
- **Applies / fails when:** Applies with real experience under time pressure and shifting conditions. Fails for novices facing genuinely unfamiliar situations (then deliberation is the fallback) and against an intelligent adversary who can exploit your predictable first move.
- **Example:** Lieutenant M diagnoses the torn artery on the run, applies pressure, skips further exam — many decisions, seconds each, zero comparison.

### [SOP:02] Singular evaluation, not comparison  (ch 3)
- **Essence:** Experts evaluate options one at a time on their own merits (singular evaluation / satisficing), not by comparing strengths and weaknesses side by side (comparative evaluation / optimizing). They take the first option that works, not the best.
- **Decision move:** Generate one plausible action, test it by simulation; if it fails, move to the next-most-typical, don't lay out a matrix. Stop at "good enough."
- **Applies / fails when:** Strongest under time pressure and when options are close in value (the "zone of indifference" — harder to choose, less it matters). Comparison is warranted when you must justify the choice or truly optimize.
- **Example:** The overpass-rescue lieutenant cycles through rescue harness → strap → ladder belt, rejecting each by imagining it, never comparing two at once.

### [SOP:03] Intuition is recognized patterns, not magic  (ch 4)
- **Essence:** Intuition is the ability to use accumulated experience to recognize patterns and judge a situation typical or atypical — often without being able to articulate the cues. It grows from experience; it is not an inborn gift.
- **Decision move:** Trust the felt sense of an experienced practitioner, but make the cues explicit where you can (build cue lists, train on cases). To grow intuition, expand the case base, not the rulebook.
- **Applies / fails when:** Reliable in domains with rich, repeated, feedback-bearing experience. Suspect when the "gut feel" comes from thin or feedback-poor experience.
- **Example:** NICU nurses spot sepsis in microbabies from subtle cue patterns (a baby that stops crying) they can't verbalize until interviewed — premature babies become *less* irritable, the opposite of adults.

### [SOP:04] Expectancies as your tripwire  (ch 4)
- **Essence:** Recognizing a situation generates expectancies about what should happen next. When events violate them, the experienced decider notices the anomaly and re-diagnoses — a built-in error check, the opposite of confirmation bias.
- **Decision move:** State what you expect to see if your read is correct; treat any violated expectancy as a signal to stop and rescope, not to explain away.
- **Applies / fails when:** Works when expectancies are clear and you stay honest about violations. Fails when you rationalize each mismatch (see [SOP:06]).
- **Example:** The "sixth sense" fire — the floor was too hot and too quiet for a kitchen fire; violated expectancies (not ESP) made the lieutenant pull his crew out seconds before the floor collapsed.

### [SOP:05] Mental simulation to evaluate and explain  (ch 5)
- **Essence:** Mental simulation imagines a sequence of states unfolding — used to project a course of action into the future (to find flaws) or to explain how the present arose. Constrained by working memory: typically ~3 variables and ~6 transitions.
- **Decision move:** Before committing, run the action step-by-step in your head looking for the place it breaks; keep the simulation lean (few moving parts, right altitude of abstraction).
- **Applies / fails when:** Requires enough domain knowledge to build the simulation (novices can't — see the Polish economy experts vs. non-experts). Fails when parts interact heavily or there are too many of them.
- **Example:** The car-rescue commander mentally lifts off the roof, supports the neck, slides the victim out — spots no flaw, executes, and it works.

### [SOP:06] The de minimis / garden-path trap  (ch 5)
- **Essence:** Mental simulation's main danger: you can explain away any contradictory evidence one piece at a time, each step plausible, until you are far off the road and don't notice. "Snap-back" only comes when the accumulated strain finally breaks the story.
- **Decision move:** Track how much disconfirming evidence you've explained away. When the count climbs, stop patching and deliberately build an alternative explanation.
- **Applies / fails when:** A risk in any prolonged diagnosis or monitoring task, worst under low experience and high commitment to one hypothesis.
- **Example:** Two physicists "see" a dock on Crater Lake (which has none) and conclude "they must have built it since this morning" rather than admit they're lost.

### [SOP:07] Premortem and crystal ball  (ch 5)
- **Essence:** Two debiasing uses of mental simulation. Premortem: imagine the plan has already failed and explain why — this breaks emotional attachment and surfaces hidden flaws in minutes. Crystal ball: assume your confident explanation is wrong and generate an alternative.
- **Decision move:** At plan kickoff, ask the team "it's months later and this failed — why?" For diagnoses, ask "if this read is wrong, what else could it be?"
- **Applies / fails when:** Best when there's time to reflect (not in the heat of action) and when overconfidence in a single plan/explanation is the risk.
- **Example:** Royal Dutch/Shell's decision scenarios — three futures including a "three-miracle" version of the executives' own assumptions — let them anticipate the 1973 oil shock.

### [SOP:08] The decision-bias post-hoc fallacy  (ch 6)
- **Essence:** Labeling a wrong call as "expectancy bias" or "scenario fulfillment" after the fact explains too much — whichever way it had gone, a bias could be named. An explanation that fits every outcome is no explanation; look for concrete causes (bad data, bad interfaces).
- **Decision move:** When reviewing a failure, reject the reflex bias label; reconstruct what information and interface the decider actually had and find the specific breakdown.
- **Applies / fails when:** Applies to any blame-the-operator post-mortem. The real culprits are usually latent: poor design, poor data, missing trend displays.
- **Example:** The USS *Vincennes* shootdown — crew "saw the airliner descending" not from bias but from a recycled track number pointing at a different, descending aircraft, plus an unreadable altitude display.

### [SOP:09] Match strategy to task conditions  (ch 7)
- **Essence:** RPD/singular evaluation and rational comparison are both legitimate; which fits depends on the task. Singular: greater time pressure, higher experience, dynamic conditions, ill-defined goals. Comparative: need to justify, conflict resolution among stakeholders, optimization, high computational complexity.
- **Decision move:** Diagnose the conditions first. Reach for analysis only when you must defend the choice, reconcile parties, find the true best, or the math genuinely exceeds intuition.
- **Applies / fails when:** A meta-rule for every decision. The error is defaulting to one mode regardless of context.
- **Example:** The Denver bullets case — multiple stakeholders with conflicting values needed a common analytical metric, so formal multi-attribute analysis (rightly) resolved it.

### [SOP:10] Learn like an expert  (ch 7)
- **Essence:** Since you can't quickly teach people to *think* like experts, teach them to *learn* like experts: deliberate practice with goals and criteria, an extensive experience bank, accurate/diagnostic/timely feedback, and structured reflection on past cases.
- **Decision move:** Identify the key recurring judgments (decision requirements), engineer practice and feedback around them, and review decisions for the thinking — not just the outcome.
- **Applies / fails when:** Works where feedback is obtainable (weather forecasting). Mere experience does NOT build expertise where feedback is poor (clinical psychology, policy).
- **Example:** Marine squad-leader training — decision-requirement exercises, tactical decision games, and cognitive critiques replaced formal-analysis lessons and boosted confidence.

### [SOP:11] Leverage points and choke points  (ch 8)
- **Essence:** A leverage point is a small difference that makes a large difference — a foothold for constructing a new course of action. Choke points are the mirror: small vulnerabilities that can collapse your plan. Experts notice both; novices see only the physical layout.
- **Decision move:** Instead of pushing against the whole problem, scan for the opening that shifts the center of gravity. Equally, hunt for the single point of failure and pre-empt it before any sign of trouble.
- **Applies / fails when:** Valuable across business, military (coup d'oeil), science, rescue. Requires expertise — the leverage point is invisible without it.
- **Example:** The physician finds an airway in an infant's cyst-blocked throat by *making bubbles* (pressing the chest) — the leverage point recalled from an analogue case.

### [SOP:12] Nonlinear problem solving  (ch 9)
- **Essence:** Most real problems are ill-defined: experienced people disagree on what counts as solved. You clarify the goal *while* generating and evaluating courses of action — failures teach you about the goal. Detection, representation, option generation, and evaluation interact; they aren't sequential stages.
- **Decision move:** Don't freeze trying to fully define the goal first. Let the goal evolve through attempts; treat each failed option as information about what the goal really requires.
- **Applies / fails when:** Applies to ill-defined goals (writing, design, rescue, planning). The classic four-stage "define → generate → evaluate → execute" model interferes here.
- **Example:** *Apollo 13* — problem representation was the dominant activity (31 of 73 episodes); the goal shifted from completing the mission to returning the crew alive as understanding grew.

### [SOP:13] See the invisible  (ch 10)
- **Essence:** Expertise is learning to perceive, not just accumulating facts. Experts see patterns, anomalies and *missing events* (negative cues — the dog that didn't bark), the big picture, how things work, the past and future, fine discriminations, and their own limits (metacognition).
- **Decision move:** When you need expert judgment, probe what the expert *notices* — especially absences and trends — and capture it via cognitive task analysis rather than codifying only procedures.
- **Applies / fails when:** Perceptual expertise is real and trainable but slow; it doesn't transfer by being told. Pretenders have smooth routines but can't improvise when pushed off the standard pattern.
- **Example:** Chess masters keep their move quality and ~7% blunder rate nearly constant even under blitz (6 sec/move); class B players' blunders jump from 11% to 25%.

### [SOP:14] Stories as packaged experience  (ch 11)
- **Essence:** Stories organize events into causal frameworks and act as natural experiments — "under these conditions, this is what happened." They carry many interacting causes at once (richness over precision) and serve as vicarious experience. They're judged like mental simulations: plausibility, consistency, economy, uniqueness.
- **Decision move:** To extract tacit expertise, ask for specific tough cases (the critical decision method), not general questions. To transmit a lesson, embed cues in a story, not a bullet list of slogans.
- **Applies / fails when:** Powerful for learning, diagnosis, and knowledge elicitation. A weak/over-patched story (too many explained-away anomalies) signals a bad hypothesis — reject it.
- **Example:** The pneumopericardium nurse acts on a story she lived once before (the monitor shows electrical activity but no actual heartbeat) and saves the baby by overriding the team.

### [SOP:15] Reason by analogue, adjust on the knowable  (ch 12)
- **Essence:** Analogues (and metaphors) let you predict and decide when you can't enumerate all causal factors — the analogue silently carries the unknown causes and their interactions. Select the analogue on the deep "mysterious" causal dynamics; adjust on the measurable differences (size, pressure). Similarity is meaningless without purpose.
- **Decision move:** Pick a case matching the causal dynamics of yours, then scale the data for the knowable differences. If a key difference can't be reduced to a proportion, reject the analogue even if it looks identical.
- **Applies / fails when:** Most useful in the middle zone — you know something but not enough for formulas. With deep knowledge use formulas; with little, analogues mislead. Ground it in hard data, not invented estimates.
- **Example:** The engineer rejects the *identical* C-5A auxiliary-power-unit data for the B-1 because a bomber's scramble/maneuver demands differ unadjustably from a cargo plane's.

### [SOP:16] Communicate intent, not just instructions  (ch 13)
- **Essence:** Every request requires the receiver to "read your mind." Giving the *why* (intent) — not a choreographed sequence of steps — lets people improvise correctly when the plan meets reality, set priorities, catch your errors, and act without waiting. Seven facets: purpose, objective image, plan, rationale, key decisions, antigoals, constraints.
- **Decision move:** When delegating, state the higher purpose and a picture of success; leave the *how* to the doer. Use the seven facets as a checklist; give as little as possible without obscuring the point.
- **Applies / fails when:** With experienced teams, emphasize high-level goals; with novices, specify more steps. Over-specifying time/resources is micromanagement that kills the flexibility intent is meant to enable.
- **Example:** Churchill's "do not be brought to action against superior forces" (meant to dodge the Austrians) was read literally by Troubridge, who let the *Goeben* escape — a missing rationale cost an empire.

### [SOP:17] Guard against hyperrationality  (ch 15)
- **Essence:** Rational analysis is a genuine source of power (decompose, decontextualize, calculate, describe) with real limits. Hyperrationality — forcing analysis everywhere — fails because there are no natural "basic elements," rules are ambiguous (the hard part is judging when the "if" is met), estimates are subjective, and inference chains explode combinatorially.
- **Decision move:** Use experiential sources to frame the situation and reach a manageable representation; bring in analysis to add precision where it fits. Don't decompose a problem that has no stable parts. Prefer consistency of *function* over consistency of *feature*.
- **Applies / fails when:** Analysis dominates a few tasks (statistics, complex tradeoffs), plays a partial role in many, and none in some. Beware: forcing analysis can degrade intuitive performance.
- **Example:** Galton's self-induced paranoia experiment — pure logical consistency, untethered from reasonableness, spiraled out of control; logic is indifferent to truth.

### [SOP:18] Poor decisions come from thin experience, not warped minds  (ch 16)
- **Essence:** Across 600+ decision points, errors traced mostly to (1) lack of experience, (2) lack of information, and (3) the de minimis error — not to inherent decision biases. Stress doesn't warp choices; it limits the information and working memory you can bring to bear. Uncertainty is "doubt that threatens to block action."
- **Decision move:** When decisions go wrong, fix experience, information access, and interfaces — don't just train against named biases. Accept that errors are inevitable; make malfunctions *visible* so operators can catch and correct them, rather than burying them under defenses.
- **Applies / fails when:** Applies wherever feedback exists to build expertise. Where the domain is dynamic, human-behavior-dependent, low-feedback, and low-repetition, "experience" yields smooth routines but not real expertise (and risks superstition).
- **Example:** The missed NICU diagnosis — the nurse explains away each sign of necrotizing enterocolitis (big belly = family trait, small blood, small aspirate) until the baby is critically ill the next day.
